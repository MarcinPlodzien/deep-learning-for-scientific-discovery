Lab 004 — Automatic differentiation

Training a neural network means minimising a loss function \(\mathcal L(\vec\theta)\) over parameters \(\vec\theta\), iteratively, by gradient descent: \[\vec\theta_{t+1} = \vec\theta_t - \eta\,\frac{\partial\mathcal L(\vec\theta_t)}{\partial\vec\theta}. \tag{1}\] Here \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\vec\theta\) is the gradient, the vector of all partial derivatives \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial\theta_i\). It points in the direction in which \(\mathcal L\) increases fastest, so a small step against it lowers the loss. The positive step size \(\eta\) is called the learning rate. Modern networks have \(10^6\)–\(10^{12}\) parameters. The central technical problem is therefore:

How can the gradient of a complicated function of up to \(10^{12}\) variables be computed exactly and at low cost?

The answer is automatic differentiation (AD). AD is also useful in physics on its own: it differentiates energies, observables, or the result of a matrix diagonalisation with respect to Hamiltonian parameters.

We first compare three ways of computing derivatives (finite differences, symbolic differentiation and AD) and then look at the structure that makes AD possible, the computational graph. On this graph we build the two modes of AD ourselves: forward mode, based on dual numbers, and reverse mode, which is backpropagation. Next we meet the same machinery in torch.autograd, use it to differentiate through a matrix diagonalisation in a quantum-mechanical problem, and turn gradients into optimisation algorithms. The inverse Schrödinger problem at the end combines everything: a potential is optimised through the Schrödinger equation in the same way a network is trained. A summary and exercises with solutions follow.

1. Three ways to compute derivatives

1.1 Finite differences

From the definition of the derivative, for a small step \(h\), the forward difference \[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} \approx \frac{f(\vec x + h\vec e_i) - f(\vec x)}{h} \tag{2}\] has an error of order \(h\), and the central difference \[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} \approx \frac{f(\vec x + h\vec e_i) - f(\vec x - h\vec e_i)}{2h} \tag{3}\] an error of order \(h^2\). Here \(\vec e_i\) is the unit vector along axis \(i\) (only \(x_i\) is changed), and “an error of order \(h\)” means that the error is proportional to \(h\) for small \(h\). The method has two problems. The first is its cost: a gradient in \(m\) dimensions needs \(\mathcal O(m)\) evaluations of \(f\), which is impossible for \(10^9\) parameters. The second is its accuracy. The computer stores \(f(x+h)\) and \(f(x)\) with a relative rounding error of about the machine epsilon \(\epsilon_\text{machine}\). For small \(h\) the two values are nearly equal; their difference keeps an absolute error of about \(\epsilon_\text{machine}|f|\), and dividing by \(h\) enlarges it. This round-off error therefore grows in inverse proportion to \(h\). There is an optimal \(h\), and it is not known in advance.

The accuracy problem is visible already for the sine function at the point 1 (exact derivative \(\cos 1\)), with both formulas evaluated for 100 values of \(h\) between \(10^{-16}\) and 1 and the absolute error plotted on log–log axes. The two dotted and dashed reference lines show only the slopes, \[e_{\rm round}(h)=\frac{10^{-16}}{h},\qquad e_{\rm trunc}(h)=0.5\,h . \tag{4}\] The round-off line uses a constant of the order of the machine precision, about \(2.2\times10^{-16}\), and the truncation line a constant close to the prefactor of the forward-difference error, \[\frac{|f''(1)|}{2} = \frac{\sin 1}{2} \approx 0.42 . \tag{5}\]

Code 
f, df_exact, x0 = np.sin, np.cos(1.0), 1.0
hs = np.logspace(-16, 0, 100)
err_fwd = np.abs((f(x0 + hs) - f(x0)) / hs - df_exact)
err_cen = np.abs((f(x0 + hs) - f(x0 - hs)) / (2 * hs) - df_exact)

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4.5))
ax.loglog(hs, err_fwd, label="forward difference")
ax.loglog(hs, err_cen, label="central difference")
ax.loglog(hs, 1e-16 / hs, "k:", lw=1, label=r"round-off $\sim\epsilon/h$")
ax.loglog(hs, 0.5 * hs, "k--", lw=1, label=r"truncation $\sim h$")
ax.set_xlabel("step $h$"); ax.set_ylabel("absolute error of $f'(1)$")
ax.set_ylim(1e-13, 1); ax.legend()
plt.show()

The plot is read from the right. For large \(h\) the error decreases along the truncation line (linearly in \(h\) for the forward and quadratically for the central difference). Below the optimum it grows again along the round-off line, and the curves become jagged because rounding errors behave like random numbers. The best achievable accuracy is about \(10^{-9}\) (forward, at a step near \(10^{-8}\)) or \(10^{-11}\)–\(10^{-12}\) (central, at a step near \(10^{-6}\)–\(10^{-5}\)), far from machine precision, of order \(10^{-16}\). The position of the optimum follows from adding the two errors. The Taylor expansion \[f(x+h) = f + hf' + \tfrac12h^2f'' + \tfrac16h^3f''' + \dots \tag{6}\] gives the truncation error of the forward formula, proportional to \(f''\); in the central formula the \(f''\) terms of \(f(x+h)\) and \(f(x-h)\) cancel, and the first remaining term is proportional to \(f'''\). For the forward difference the total error is about \[e_{\rm fwd}(h)\approx\frac{\epsilon}{h} + \frac{|f''|\,h}{2}, \tag{7}\] smallest at \[h\sim\sqrt{\epsilon}\approx10^{-8}. \tag{8}\] For the central difference it is about \[e_{\rm cen}(h)\approx\frac{\epsilon}{h} + \frac{|f'''|\,h^2}{6}, \tag{9}\] smallest at \[h\sim\epsilon^{1/3}\approx10^{-5}. \tag{10}\] Both estimates agree with the positions of the minima in the plot to within an order of magnitude.

1.2 Symbolic differentiation

Computer algebra (Mathematica, sympy) applies the rules of calculus to expressions. The result is exact and interpretable, but expressions can grow enormously (“expression swell”) and the approach does not work for functions defined by programs (loops, if statements, matrix diagonalisation…).

1.3 Automatic differentiation

AD sits in between: every program — however complicated — is a composition of elementary operations (\(+,\times,\sin,\exp,\log,\dots\)) whose derivatives are known. AD records these operations and combines their local derivatives with the chain rule, giving derivatives that are exact to machine precision at a cost of a small multiple of evaluating \(f\) itself.

2. The computational graph

Consider the following function (adapted from the example in Baydin et al., Automatic differentiation in machine learning: a survey, JMLR 2018): \[y = f(x_1, x_2) = \ln x_1 + \cos x_2 - x_1 x_2 . \tag{11}\] A computational graph is a diagram of a calculation: each node is one intermediate value, and an arrow from \(v_a\) to \(v_b\) means that \(v_a\) is used to compute \(v_b\). We break the function into elementary steps. Following Baydin et al., the inputs get the indices \(-1\) and \(0\), so that the intermediate variables are numbered \(v_1,\dots,v_5\):

The forward evaluation at the point \((2, 1)\) reads the graph from left to right: \[ \begin{aligned} v_{-1} &= x_1 = 2,\\ v_0 &= x_2 = 1,\\ v_1 &= \ln v_{-1} = \ln 2 = 0.693,\\ v_2 &= v_{-1}\times v_0 = 2,\\ v_3 &= \cos v_0 = \cos 1 = 0.540,\\ v_4 &= v_1 - v_2 = -1.307,\\ y = v_5 &= v_3 + v_4 = -0.767 . \end{aligned} \tag{12} \] Analytically, \[\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} = \frac{1}{x_1} - x_2 = -0.5,\qquad \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} = -\sin x_2 - x_1 = -2.841; \tag{13}\] AD must reproduce these numbers.

3. Forward-mode AD and dual numbers

Forward mode carries along with every intermediate value \(v_i\) its derivative with respect to one chosen input, \[\dot v_i = \frac{\partial v_i}{\partial x_1}. \tag{14}\] Each elementary step updates both by the chain rule. The primal is the ordinary value and the tangent the derivative carried along with it. The seed is the derivative we give to the inputs at the start: 1 for the input we differentiate with respect to, and 0 for the other one. At the point \((2,1)\), primals on the left and tangents on the right: \[ \begin{aligned} v_{-1} &= x_1 = 2, & \dot v_{-1} &= 1 \ \text{(seed)},\\ v_0 &= x_2 = 1, & \dot v_0 &= 0,\\ v_1 &= \ln v_{-1}, & \dot v_1 &= \dot v_{-1}/v_{-1} = 0.5,\\ v_2 &= v_{-1}v_0, & \dot v_2 &= \dot v_{-1}v_0 + v_{-1}\dot v_0 = 1,\\ v_3 &= \cos v_0, & \dot v_3 &= -\dot v_0\sin v_0 = 0,\\ v_4 &= v_1 - v_2, & \dot v_4 &= \dot v_1 - \dot v_2 = -0.5,\\ v_5 &= v_3 + v_4, & \dot v_5 &= \dot v_3 + \dot v_4 = -0.5 . \end{aligned} \tag{15} \] The last tangent agrees with \(\partial f/\partial x_1\) of equation (13).

Dual numbers make this automatic. Extend the real numbers by an element \(\varepsilon\) that squares to zero (like the imaginary unit, but nilpotent): \[\varepsilon^2 = 0,\qquad z = a + b\,\varepsilon . \tag{16}\] Then \[(a_1 + b_1\varepsilon) + (a_2 + b_2\varepsilon) = (a_1+a_2) + (b_1+b_2)\varepsilon,\qquad (a_1 + b_1\varepsilon)(a_2 + b_2\varepsilon) = a_1a_2 + (a_1b_2 + b_1a_2)\varepsilon, \tag{17}\] and from the Taylor series \[g(a + b\varepsilon) = g(a) + g'(a)\,b\varepsilon + \tfrac12 g''(a)\,b^2\varepsilon^2 + \dots, \tag{18}\] in which every term containing \(\varepsilon^2\) vanishes, \[g(a + b\varepsilon) = g(a) + g'(a)\,b\,\varepsilon . \tag{19}\] So evaluating \(f\) with the dual number \(x_1+\varepsilon\) in place of \(x_1\) returns \[f(x_1+\varepsilon, x_2) = f(x_1,x_2) + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1}\varepsilon : \tag{20}\] the coefficient of \(\varepsilon\) in the result is the derivative. In code this is a small class that uses operator overloading: defining __add__ and __mul__ makes + and * work on Dual objects. A plain number such as 3 is lifted to Dual(3, 0) (a constant has derivative zero), and __radd__, __rmul__ handle the case where the plain number stands on the left, as in 3 + Dual(...).

Code 
class Dual:
    """Dual number a + b*eps with eps^2 = 0.  'a' = value, 'b' = derivative."""

    def __init__(self, a, b=0.0):
        self.a, self.b = a, b

    @staticmethod
    def lift(z):                      # turn plain numbers into duals with zero derivative
        return z if isinstance(z, Dual) else Dual(z, 0.0)

    def __add__(self, other):
        other = Dual.lift(other)
        return Dual(self.a + other.a, self.b + other.b)

    def __sub__(self, other):
        other = Dual.lift(other)
        return Dual(self.a - other.a, self.b - other.b)

    def __mul__(self, other):         # product rule
        other = Dual.lift(other)
        return Dual(self.a * other.a, self.a * other.b + self.b * other.a)

    __radd__ = __add__                # so that 3 + Dual(...) also works
    __rmul__ = __mul__

    def __repr__(self):
        return f"Dual(value={self.a:.6f}, derivative={self.b:.6f})"

# elementary functions: g(a + b eps) = g(a) + g'(a) b eps
def d_log(z): return Dual(math.log(z.a), z.b / z.a)
def d_cos(z): return Dual(math.cos(z.a), -math.sin(z.a) * z.b)
def d_sin(z): return Dual(math.sin(z.a), math.cos(z.a) * z.b)

For the function of Section 2, the derivative with respect to \(x_1\) is obtained by giving \(x_1\) the derivative part 1 and \(x_2\) the derivative part 0, and vice versa.

Code 
def f_dual(x1, x2):
    return d_log(x1) + d_cos(x2) - x1 * x2

# derivative w.r.t. x1: seed x1 with derivative 1, x2 with 0
print("d/dx1:", f_dual(Dual(2.0, 1.0), Dual(1.0, 0.0)))
# derivative w.r.t. x2: a SECOND forward pass with a different seed
print("d/dx2:", f_dual(Dual(2.0, 0.0), Dual(1.0, 1.0)))
print("exact:", 1/2 - 1, -math.sin(1) - 2)

d/dx2: Dual(value=-0.766551, derivative=-2.841471)

Both passes return the value \(-0.7666\) of equation (12) together with one derivative each, and both derivatives equal the exact results.

Forward mode needs one pass per input variable. It is therefore efficient for functions with few inputs and many outputs, and inefficient for a loss with \(10^9\) inputs and a single output, which would need \(10^9\) passes.

4. Reverse-mode AD and backpropagation

Reverse mode first runs the program forward and records the graph. Then it goes backwards from the output, computing the adjoints \[\bar v_i = \frac{\partial y}{\partial v_i}. \tag{21}\] The chain rule says that the adjoint of a node is a sum over all nodes \(v_j\) that use it: \[\bar v_i = \sum_{j\,:\,v_i\to v_j}\bar v_j\,\frac{\partial v_j}{\partial v_i}. \tag{22}\] Starting from the output, at the point \((2,1)\): \[ \begin{aligned} \bar v_5 &= \frac{\partial y}{\partial y} = 1,\\ \bar v_4 &= \bar v_5\,\frac{\partial v_5}{\partial v_4} = \bar v_5 = 1,\\ \bar v_3 &= \bar v_5\,\frac{\partial v_5}{\partial v_3} = \bar v_5 = 1,\\ \bar v_2 &= \bar v_4\,\frac{\partial v_4}{\partial v_2} = -\bar v_4 = -1,\\ \bar v_1 &= \bar v_4\,\frac{\partial v_4}{\partial v_1} = \bar v_4 = 1,\\ \bar v_0 &= \bar v_3\,(-\sin v_0) + \bar v_2\, v_{-1} = -0.841 - 2 = -2.841,\\ \bar v_{-1} &= \bar v_1 / v_{-1} + \bar v_2\, v_0 = 0.5 - 1 = -0.5 . \end{aligned} \tag{23} \] The last two lines agree with equation (13).

One backward pass gives the derivatives with respect to all inputs. Cost: a small constant times one evaluation of \(f\), independent of the number of parameters. This is why reverse mode, called backpropagation in the neural-network language, is the method used to train neural networks.

A minimal implementation of what PyTorch does internally (in the spirit of A. Karpathy’s micrograd) fits in a few lines. Each Var stores its value, the Vars it was computed from (parents) and the local derivatives with respect to them. The method backward must visit the nodes in an order in which every node comes after all nodes it depends on (a topological order). The inner function build produces it by a depth-first traversal: it first calls itself on all parents and only then appends the node, and a node used twice, such as \(x_1\), is placed only once. The reversed list visits the output first and the inputs last.

Code 
class Var:
    """A scalar that remembers how it was computed, so that gradients can flow back."""

    def __init__(self, value, parents=(), local_grads=(), name=""):
        self.value = value
        self.grad = 0.0                  # will hold dy/d(this) after backward()
        self.parents = parents           # the Vars this one was computed from
        self.local_grads = local_grads   # d(this)/d(parent) for each parent
        self.name = name

    def __add__(self, other): return Var(self.value + other.value, (self, other), (1.0, 1.0))
    def __sub__(self, other): return Var(self.value - other.value, (self, other), (1.0, -1.0))
    def __mul__(self, other): return Var(self.value * other.value, (self, other), (other.value, self.value))

    def backward(self):
        # 1) order the graph so that every node comes after all nodes it depends on (topological sort)
        order, visited = [], set()
        def build(v):
            if id(v) not in visited:
                visited.add(id(v))
                for p in v.parents:
                    build(p)
                order.append(v)
        build(self)
        # 2) seed and propagate adjoints from the output back to the inputs
        self.grad = 1.0
        for v in reversed(order):
            for parent, local in zip(v.parents, v.local_grads):
                parent.grad += v.grad * local     # chain rule; '+=' sums over all paths

def v_log(v): return Var(math.log(v.value), (v,), (1.0 / v.value,))
def v_cos(v): return Var(math.cos(v.value), (v,), (-math.sin(v.value),))

With Var objects the forward pass builds the graph, and one backward pass gives both derivatives.

Code 
x1, x2 = Var(2.0, name="x1"), Var(1.0, name="x2")
y = v_log(x1) + v_cos(x2) - x1 * x2       # forward pass builds the graph
y.backward()                              # ONE backward pass
print(f"y = {y.value:.4f}")
print(f"dy/dx1 = {x1.grad:.4f},  dy/dx2 = {x2.grad:.4f}")
y = -0.7666
dy/dx1 = -0.5000,  dy/dx2 = -2.8415

5. torch.autograd

PyTorch implements exactly this reverse-mode machinery for tensors and hundreds of operations (including matmul, eigh, fft, …). Setting requires_grad=True marks a tensor as a variable we want derivatives with respect to. Every operation on such a tensor records a node of the graph (grad_fn), and y.backward() runs the backward pass. The gradients land in .grad of the leaf tensors. A leaf is a tensor created directly by us (here x1 and x2), not as the result of an operation; the leaves are the inputs of the graph.

Code 
x1 = torch.tensor(2.0, requires_grad=True)
x2 = torch.tensor(1.0, requires_grad=True)

y = torch.log(x1) + torch.cos(x2) - x1 * x2
print("y       :", y)                     # grad_fn=<SubBackward0>: the last operation recorded in the graph
y.backward()
print("dy/dx1  :", x1.grad.item())      # .item() converts a one-element tensor to a Python number
print("dy/dx2  :", x2.grad.item())
y       : tensor(-0.7666, grad_fn=<SubBackward0>)
dy/dx1  : -0.5
dy/dx2  : -2.8414709848078967

The recorded graph can be traversed backwards from y.grad_fn. It contains the same elementary operations as the graph in Section 2; the grouping differs because Python evaluates log(x1) + cos(x2) first and subtracts x1*x2 last.

Code 
def show_graph(fn, depth=0):
    # Print the name of each graph node, indented by its distance from the output.
    # fn.next_functions lists the nodes that fn received its inputs from (pairs (node, index));

        return

    for parent, _ in fn.next_functions:
        show_graph(parent, depth + 1)

5.1 Derivatives of a whole curve at once

backward() starts from a single number (a scalar), so for a tensor \(x\) of many points we sum the outputs first. Since each term of the sum depends only on its own point, \[\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}\sum_j f(x_j) = f'(x_i): \tag{24}\] the gradient is the derivative at every point. Here the function is \[f(x) = e^{-x^2/2}\sin 3x , \tag{25}\] and its derivative is compared with the analytic result.

Code 
x = torch.linspace(-4, 4, 400, requires_grad=True)
y = torch.exp(-x**2 / 2) * torch.sin(3 * x)
y.sum().backward()

xd = x.detach()                                   # detach: a plain tensor without graph, for plotting
analytic = torch.exp(-xd**2 / 2) * (3 * torch.cos(3 * xd) - xd * torch.sin(3 * xd))
print("max |autograd - analytic| =", (x.grad - analytic).abs().max().item())

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.plot(xd, y.detach(), label="$f(x)$")
ax.plot(xd, x.grad, label="$f'(x)$  (autograd)")
ax.plot(xd[::15], analytic[::15], "k.", label="$f'(x)$  (analytic)")
ax.set_xlabel("$x$"); ax.legend(); plt.show()
max |autograd - analytic| = 4.440892098500626e-16

The largest difference, about \(4\times10^{-16}\), is at the level of float64 rounding: the autograd derivative is exact up to machine precision.

5.2 Higher derivatives

The function torch.autograd.grad(y, x) returns the gradient instead of storing it in .grad. With create_graph=True the gradient computation itself is recorded, so it can be differentiated again. A physics example is a potential \(V(x)\), from which follow the force \[F = -V'(x) \tag{26}\] and the curvature \(V''(x)\); the curvature at the minimum gives the vibration frequency \[\omega = \sqrt{V''(r_0)/\mu}, \tag{27}\] where \(\mu\) is the reduced mass. Take the Morse potential \[V(r) = D\,\big(1 - e^{-a(r - r_0)}\big)^2, \tag{28}\] a model of the bond between two atoms (\(D\) is the depth of the well, \(r_0\) the bond length, \(a\) sets the width), for which \[V''(r_0) = 2Da^2 \tag{29}\] (the values of \(D\), \(a\), \(r_0\) below are arbitrary).

torch.autograd.grad returns a tuple with one gradient per input tensor, even when there is only one input.

Code 
D, a, r0 = 4.0, 1.5, 1.0
r = torch.tensor(r0, requires_grad=True)

(d2V,) = torch.autograd.grad(dV, r)                    # second derivative

print(f"V''(r0) = {d2V.item():.6f}   exact 2 D a^2 = {2*D*a**2}")

5.3 Four common pitfalls

Four mistakes with autograd are common. The first is that gradients accumulate: every call of backward() adds the new gradient to .grad instead of overwriting it. The derivative of \(w^2\) at the point 3 is 6, but three backward passes without a reset give 6, 12 and 18. Every training loop must therefore reset the gradients before each backward pass, with w.grad = None or optimizer.zero_grad() (Section 7).

Code 
# Pitfall 1: gradients ACCUMULATE. Calling backward twice adds up the gradients.
w = torch.tensor(3.0, requires_grad=True)
for i in range(3):
    loss = w**2
    loss.backward()
    print(f"iteration {i}: w.grad = {w.grad.item()}   (true gradient: 6)")
# -> every training loop must reset them: w.grad = None  or  optimizer.zero_grad()  (Section 7)
iteration 0: w.grad = 6.0   (true gradient: 6)
iteration 1: w.grad = 12.0   (true gradient: 6)
iteration 2: w.grad = 18.0   (true gradient: 6)

The second is that the graph is freed after backward() to save memory, so a second gradient needs a new forward pass, which every training step makes anyway.

The fourth concerns parameter updates written directly in the code, without an optimiser. The update itself is an operation on w; if it were recorded, the graph would keep growing from one step to the next. Inside with torch.no_grad(): nothing is recorded. The update must also be in place (w -= ...): it changes the numbers stored in the existing tensor w, which stays the same leaf tensor with requires_grad=True. Writing w = w - 0.1 * w.grad would instead create a new tensor and bind the name w to it. Inside no_grad the new tensor does not require gradients at all; outside no_grad it is not a leaf and its .grad stays None after the next backward pass. In both cases the training stops working.

Code 
# Pitfall 4: parameter updates must NOT be recorded in the graph -> wrap them in torch.no_grad()
w = torch.tensor(3.0, requires_grad=True)
loss = w**2
loss.backward()

    w -= 0.1 * w.grad          # an in-place update of a leaf, outside the graph
w.grad = None                  # reset for the next step (pitfall 1)

6. Differentiating through a diagonalisation

Consider the double-well potential (harmonic trap and Gaussian barrier of height \(\kappa\)): \[\hat H(\kappa) = -\frac{1}{2}\frac{d^2}{dx^2} + \frac{1}{2}x^2 + \kappa\, e^{-x^2/2}. \tag{30}\] We ask how the energies \(E_n(\kappa)\) respond to changes of the barrier, that is, we want the derivatives \(dE_n/d\kappa\).

The program is: build the matrix \(\bar H(\kappa)\) (the bar denotes the matrix of \(\hat H\) on the grid) → diagonalise → pick eigenvalue \(n\). No formula for \(E_n(\kappa)\) is available, but every step (including torch.linalg.eigh) is differentiable, so autograd gives \(dE_n/d\kappa\) exactly (up to the grid discretisation).

Two wells appear because \[V'(x) = x\,\big(1 - \kappa e^{-x^2/2}\big), \tag{31}\] \[V''(0) = 1-\kappa . \tag{32}\] For a barrier height above 1 the origin becomes a maximum, and two minima appear at \[x_{\min} = \pm\sqrt{2\ln\kappa}, \tag{33}\] for example at \(\pm1.67\) for the barrier height 4.

The box \([-6, 6]\) is much wider than the low-lying states. With 300 grid points the spacing \(\Delta x\) is about 0.04, small compared with the scale (of order 1) on which these states vary. The lowest four energies at the barrier height 4 change by less than \(10^{-3}\) (relative change below \(2\times10^{-4}\)) when the number of grid points \(N_x\) is increased from 300 to 1000, so any \(N_x\) of a few hundred gives the same picture; larger \(N_x\) only makes the diagonalisation slower.

torch.linalg.eigvalsh(H) returns only the eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix (in ascending order) and does less work than torch.linalg.eigh(H), which also returns the eigenvectors.

Code 
# Grid: psi''(x_i) ~ (psi_{i+1} - 2 psi_i + psi_{i-1}) / dx^2, and psi = 0 beyond the ends of the grid
# (hard walls at x = -6 and x = 6, far from where the wavefunctions live).
L_box, Nx = 12.0, 300
x = torch.linspace(-L_box / 2, L_box / 2, Nx)
dx = x[1] - x[0]

# kinetic part (does not depend on kappa): built once
ones = torch.ones(Nx - 1)
H_kin = -0.5 / dx**2 * (torch.diag(ones, 1) - 2 * torch.eye(Nx) + torch.diag(ones, -1))

def potential(x, kappa):
    return 0.5 * x**2 + kappa * torch.exp(-x**2 / 2)

def hamiltonian(kappa):
    """Matrix of H(kappa) on the grid; differentiable w.r.t. kappa."""
    return H_kin + torch.diag(potential(x, kappa))

def energies(kappa, n_levels=4):
    return torch.linalg.eigvalsh(hamiltonian(kappa))[:n_levels]

The eigenvectors are normalised as vectors, \[\sum_i|\psi_n(x_i)|^2 = 1 ; \tag{34}\] dividing by \(\Delta x\) turns this into a probability density with \[\sum_i \frac{|\psi_n(x_i)|^2}{\Delta x}\,\Delta x = 1, \tag{35}\] the discrete form of the normalisation of the integral of \(|\psi|^2\).

For the barrier height 10 they form pairs of almost equal energy, with \[E_1 - E_0 \approx 5\times10^{-4},\qquad E_3 - E_2 \approx 9\times10^{-3}, \tag{37}\] and the two curves of each pair lie on top of each other.

A single derivative, \(dE_0/d\kappa\) at the barrier height 4 (in the double-well regime), is checked first.

Two independent computations confirm this number. The first is the Hellmann–Feynman theorem: for a normalised eigenstate, \[\frac{dE_n}{d\kappa} = \Big\langle\psi_n\Big|\frac{\partial\hat H}{\partial\kappa}\Big|\psi_n\Big\rangle = \langle\psi_n|\,e^{-x^2/2}\,|\psi_n\rangle . \tag{38}\] Here \(\langle\psi_n|\hat A|\psi_n\rangle\) is the expectation value of \(\hat A\) in the state \(\psi_n\). On the grid (eigenvectors from eigh are normalised as vectors) it is \[\frac{dE_n}{d\kappa} = \sum_i |\psi_n(x_i)|^2 e^{-x_i^2/2},\qquad \frac{d\bar H}{d\kappa} = \mathrm{diag}\big(e^{-x_i^2/2}\big). \tag{39}\] On the grid the theorem follows in one line. With \[E_n = \vec\psi_n^T\bar H\vec\psi_n,\qquad \vec\psi_n^T\vec\psi_n = 1, \tag{40}\] the product rule gives \[\frac{dE_n}{d\kappa} = \vec\psi_n^T\frac{d\bar H}{d\kappa}\vec\psi_n + 2\,\vec\psi_n^T\bar H\,\frac{d\vec\psi_n}{d\kappa}. \tag{41}\] Since \(\bar H\) is symmetric, \[\vec\psi_n^T\bar H = E_n\vec\psi_n^T, \tag{42}\] and the last term of equation (41) is \[2E_n\vec\psi_n^T \frac{d\vec\psi_n}{d\kappa} = E_n\,\frac{d(\vec\psi_n^T\vec\psi_n)}{d\kappa} = 0 . \tag{43}\] The change of the eigenvector does not contribute.

The second is a central finite difference. Section 1 found the optimal step near \(10^{-6}\)–\(10^{-5}\) for the sine. Here any step between about \(10^{-3}\) and \(10^{-5}\) gives agreement with autograd to better than \(10^{-8}\); we take the step \(10^{-4}\). Autograd and Hellmann–Feynman agree to all ten printed digits (0.3884316631), and the finite difference differs only in the last one.

The derivative in equation (38), the expectation value of the barrier shape \(e^{-x^2/2}\), has a simple meaning: it is the probability-weighted overlap of the state with the barrier. For large \(\kappa\) all four states are pushed out of the centre and \(dE_n/d\kappa\) decreases (for the level 2 only above a barrier height of about 4; below that it first increases); the pairs of levels \((0,1)\) and \((2,3)\) merge into quasi-degenerate tunnelling doublets (pairs of nearly equal energy; the two states of a pair are spread symmetrically and antisymmetrically over the two wells, and their small splitting is caused by tunnelling through the barrier) — and their derivatives become equal, because both states of a doublet see the barrier the same way.

One subtle point deserves attention. Derivatives of eigenvalues are well defined. Derivatives of eigenvectors, \[\frac{d\vec\psi_n}{d\kappa} = \sum_{m\neq n}\frac{\vec\psi_m^T\,(d\bar H/d\kappa)\,\vec\psi_n}{E_n - E_m}\,\vec\psi_m , \tag{44}\] contain energy differences in the denominator and blow up at (near-)degeneracies. PyTorch’s backward formula for eigh builds these factors for all pairs of eigenvalues, so exact degeneracies anywhere in the spectrum give inf, and inf multiplied by zero gives nan, even for pairs that do not contribute mathematically. The PyTorch documentation of eigh warns about this.

In short, any computation built from differentiable operations can be differentiated. The same mechanism that trains neural networks is used to optimise Hamiltonians, quantum circuits (the variational quantum eigensolver VQE and the quantum approximate optimisation algorithm QAOA, later in the course) and control pulses.

7. Gradient-based optimisation

7.1 Gradient descent

Minimise the Rosenbrock function \[f(x, y) = (1-x)^2 + 100\,(y - x^2)^2 , \tag{45}\] whose minimum, at \((1,1)\), lies at the bottom of a long, curved, flat valley — a simple model of a hard loss landscape.

The starting point \((-1.5, 2)\) lies on the far side of the curved valley, so the path has to follow its whole length; the 3000 steps show the slow motion along the valley, not convergence. The positions are stored with p.detach().clone(): detach() alone shares memory with p, which is changed in place, so all stored entries would end up equal to the final position.

Code 
def rosenbrock(p):
    return (1 - p[0])**2 + 100 * (p[1] - p[0]**2)**2

def gradient_descent(lr, n_steps=3000, start=(-1.5, 2.0)):
    p = torch.tensor(start, requires_grad=True)
    path = [p.detach().clone()]
    for _ in range(n_steps):
        loss = rosenbrock(p)
        loss.backward()                  # 1) gradient by autograd
        with torch.no_grad():
            p -= lr * p.grad             # 2) step downhill
        p.grad = None                    # 3) reset accumulated gradient
        path.append(p.detach().clone())
    return torch.stack(path)

Gradient descent (GD) quickly falls into the valley, then crawls slowly along it. A larger learning rate helps along the valley — but makes the steps across the steep walls unstable. The limit can be computed. Near a minimum, \(f\) is approximately quadratic with the Hessian matrix \(\partial^2 f/\partial x_i\partial x_j\) as curvature. There the gradient is approximately \[\nabla f \approx \nabla^2 f\,(\vec x - \vec x^\ast), \tag{46}\] where \(\nabla^2 f\) is the Hessian and \(\vec x^\ast\) the minimum, so one GD step maps \[\vec x - \vec x^\ast \;\to\; (\mathbf 1 - \eta\,\nabla^2 f)(\vec x - \vec x^\ast). \tag{47}\] Along an eigenvector of the Hessian with eigenvalue \(\lambda\), one step therefore multiplies the distance from the minimum by \[q = 1 - \eta\lambda . \tag{48}\] The iteration converges only if \[|1-\eta\lambda| < 1 \ \ \text{for all eigenvalues} \iff \eta < \frac{2}{\lambda_{\max}}. \tag{49}\] At the Rosenbrock minimum the eigenvalues are 0.40 and 1001.6, so \(\eta\) must stay below \[\frac{2}{1001.6} \approx 2.0\times10^{-3}. \tag{50}\] The flat direction, with the eigenvalue 0.40, then shrinks per step only by the factor \(q\) of equation (48), about 0.9992, which is why GD is slow.

The comparison uses the learning rates \(10^{-3}\), \(1.9\times10^{-3}\) (slightly below the limit, much closer to the minimum after 3000 steps) and \(2.5\times10^{-3}\) (above the limit: the iteration diverges, and the result is nan, “not a number”). The range of usable learning rates is narrow. Better optimisers address the mismatch between flat and steep directions.

7.2 Optimisers in torch.optim

PyTorch’s optimisers implement the update rule. For most optimisers the training loop has the same 4 lines (L-BFGS, used in Section 8, is an exception):

optimizer.zero_grad()   # reset gradients
loss = f(params)        # forward pass
loss.backward()         # backward pass
optimizer.step()        # update parameters

We compare four of them (see Lecture Notes, The Zoo of Optimization Algorithms). Plain stochastic gradient descent (SGD) updates the parameters with the gradient \(g\), \[\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta g . \tag{51}\] The name stochastic refers to estimating \(g\) from a random part of the training data. Here there are no data, \(g\) is exact, and SGD is plain gradient descent.

SGD with momentum keeps a velocity \(v\), a “heavy ball” that builds up speed along the valley: \[v \leftarrow \gamma v - \eta g,\qquad \theta\leftarrow\theta + v . \tag{52}\] The conventional value of \(\gamma\) is 0.9: the velocity is then an average over roughly the last \[\frac{1}{1-\gamma} = 10 \tag{53}\] gradients.

RMSprop (root mean square propagation) rescales steep and flat directions with a per-parameter step, \[\theta \leftarrow \theta - \eta\,\frac{g}{\sqrt{E[g^2]}},\qquad E[g^2] \leftarrow \rho\, E[g^2] + (1-\rho)\,g^2 , \tag{54}\] where \(E[g^2]\) is a running average of the squared gradient, kept separately for each parameter (PyTorch default for \(\rho\): 0.99).

Adam (adaptive moment estimation) combines momentum and RMSprop. Both running averages start at zero, which makes them too small in the first steps; the bias correction divides them by \[1-\beta^t , \tag{55}\] where \(t\) is the step number and \(\beta\) the decay factor of the average, by default 0.9 for the gradient and 0.999 for its square. Adam is the default choice in deep learning.

The learning rates differ between the optimisers because \(\eta\) means different things. With momentum, on a steady slope the steps add up to \[\frac{\eta}{1-\gamma} = 10\,\eta , \tag{56}\] so \(\eta\) is reduced. RMSprop and Adam divide the gradient by its typical size, so their \(\eta\) is roughly the step length in parameter space and can be much larger. Each value was chosen to give a stable run; the comparison shows typical behaviour, not the best possible setting of each method.

def run(opt_class, n_steps=3000, start=(-1.5, 2.0), **opt_kwargs):

        loss = rosenbrock(p)

        opt.step()
        path.append(p.detach().clone()); losses.append(loss.item())

8. The inverse Schrödinger problem

8.1 Inverse problems in physics

The usual (direct) problem of quantum mechanics is: given a potential \(V(x)\), find the eigenstates and energies. Many questions in physics are inverse: they ask which potential produces a prescribed state. In experiments with ultracold atoms, the trapping potential is shaped with laser beams, and one wants the potential that gives a desired density profile of the atomic cloud. In semiconductor quantum dots, gate voltages define the confining potential, and one wants electrons localised in a prescribed way. In density-functional theory, the Kohn–Sham inversion problem asks for the potential whose ground-state density equals a given density.

The simplest version concerns one particle in 1D: find \(V(x)\) such that the ground-state probability density \(|\psi_0(x)|^2\) is as close as possible to a target density \(\rho_\ast(x)\).

8.2 Loss function and L-BFGS

On the grid of Section 6 the potential is the vector of its \(N_x\) values \(V_i\) at the grid points; these are our trainable parameters (here 300 of them). For given \(V\) we build \[\bar H = \bar H_{\rm kin} + \mathrm{diag}(V), \tag{57}\] diagonalise it and take the ground state \(\psi_0\). The loss compares amplitudes: \[\mathcal L(V) = \sum_i \Big(|\psi_0(x_i)| - \sqrt{\rho_\ast(x_i)}\Big)^2\,\Delta x . \tag{58}\] The factor \(\Delta x\) makes the sum an approximation of the integral \[\mathcal L(V) \approx \int\big(|\psi_0| - \sqrt{\rho_\ast}\big)^2dx , \tag{59}\] so its value does not depend on the number of grid points. We compare \(|\psi_0|\) with \(\sqrt{\rho_\ast}\) rather than \(|\psi_0|^2\) with \(\rho_\ast\): the square root gives more weight to regions where the density is small, which otherwise hardly influence the loss. The gradient \(\partial\mathcal L/\partial V_i\) for all 300 components comes from one backward pass through eigh. We minimise with L-BFGS (limited-memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno), a quasi-Newton method. Newton’s method jumps to the minimum of the local quadratic approximation of the loss, \[\Delta\vec V = -(\nabla^2\mathcal L)^{-1}\nabla\mathcal L , \tag{60}\] with the Hessian \(\nabla^2\mathcal L\) (Section 7). Computing and inverting the \(300\times300\) Hessian at every step is expensive; a quasi-Newton method instead builds an approximation of \((\nabla^2\mathcal L)^{-1}\) from the gradients of previous steps. This makes it fast for smooth problems with exact (non-stochastic) gradients. Within one step it evaluates the loss several times (a line search: several step lengths are tried along the chosen direction), so opt.step receives a function, called closure, that recomputes the loss and its gradient. One L-BFGS step contains up to max_iter such iterations (a setting explained in 8.3).

The optimisation starts from a vanishing potential, not from a harmonic one. The backward pass of eigh contains the energy denominators of equation (44) for all pairs of eigenvalues, also those of highly excited states that do not enter the loss. In a symmetric potential with high walls at the edges of the grid, such as the harmonic potential on \([-6, 6]\), the highest states come in pairs localised at the left and right edge with numerically equal energies; a vanishing denominator then turns the gradient into nan (this is the warning of Section 6 in practice). The spectrum of the flat potential has no such degeneracies.

Two properties of the problem matter. First, \(V\) is determined only up to a constant: adding a constant \(c\) to \(V\) shifts all energies by \(c\) and leaves the states unchanged. We fix the constant by a shift: in 8.3 the potential is matched to the exact one in the high-density region, in 8.4 its minimum in that region is set to zero. Second, where the target density is practically zero, the potential has almost no influence on the loss, so it is not determined there.

8.3 Harmonic potential

For a node-less ground state the Schrödinger equation can be inverted exactly. Dividing \[-\tfrac12\psi_0'' + V\psi_0 = E_0\psi_0 \tag{61}\] by \(\psi_0\), which is allowed because a ground state without nodes vanishes nowhere, gives \[V(x) = E_0 + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\psi_0''(x)}{\psi_0(x)}. \tag{62}\] For the Gaussian \[\psi_\ast(x)\propto e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)} \tag{63}\] we have \[\frac{\psi_\ast''}{\psi_\ast} = \frac{x^2}{\sigma^4} - \frac{1}{\sigma^2},\qquad V(x) = \frac{x^2}{2\sigma^4} + \text{const}, \tag{64}\] a harmonic potential. We use this case to test the method. The target density is \[\rho_\ast = |\psi_\ast|^2 \propto e^{-x^2/\sigma^2}. \tag{65}\]

An eigensolver may return \(\psi\) or \(-\psi\) (both are eigenvectors), and the sign can change from one iteration to the next. The ground state has no nodes, so torch.abs(psi[:, 0]) removes this ambiguity. Dividing by \(\sqrt{\Delta x}\) converts the vector normalisation of eigh, equation (34), into the normalisation of equation (35). The width \(\sigma\), here 0.7, is arbitrary: any width works that is much larger than \(\Delta x\), about 0.04 (so that the grid resolves the density), and much smaller than the half-width 6 of the box (so that the density vanishes at the walls).

In torch.optim.LBFGS, lr=1 means a full quasi-Newton step; the line_search_fn="strong_wolfe" then shortens or lengthens each step so that the loss really decreases by a sufficient amount. Without the line search a fixed step is taken, and the run still converged, but to a noticeably larger loss. The parameter history_size=50 is the number of past gradients used to estimate the curvature; values from 10 to 100 give a final loss of the same order. The parameters tolerance_grad and tolerance_change are the thresholds below which L-BFGS stops. The defaults (\(10^{-7}\) and \(10^{-9}\)) are of the size of the loss reached here, and with them the optimisation stops early (after roughly 150–200 evaluations) at a loss a few times larger, so we set them so small that they never stop the run.

Each call opt.step(closure) makes up to max_iter=50 iterations, and the line search adds further loss evaluations: the default n_steps=5 outer steps make about 270 evaluations of the loss, each with one diagonalisation and one backward pass. For the Gaussian target the small n_steps costs little accuracy: with n_steps=20 (about 1200 evaluations, four times longer) the final loss decreased in our tests by less than a factor of two and the plots looked the same. Harder targets profit more from extra steps (Section 8.4). Finally, opt.step(closure) returns the loss at the beginning of the step, so to record the loss after the last step closure() is called once more at the end.

Code 
def ground_state_amplitude(V):
    # |psi_0| on the grid, normalised so that sum |psi_0|^2 dx = 1
    E, psi = torch.linalg.eigh(H_kin + torch.diag(V))
    return torch.abs(psi[:, 0]) / torch.sqrt(dx)

def invert(rho_target, n_steps=5):
    # Find V on the grid whose ground-state density is closest to rho_target (a tensor on the grid x)
    rho_target = rho_target / (rho_target.sum() * dx)            # normalise the target
    amp_target = torch.sqrt(rho_target)
    V = torch.zeros_like(x).requires_grad_(True)                 # start from a flat potential (see 'Starting point' in 8.2)
    opt = torch.optim.LBFGS([V], lr=1, max_iter=50, history_size=50, line_search_fn="strong_wolfe",
                            tolerance_grad=1e-12, tolerance_change=1e-16)
    history = []

    def closure():
        opt.zero_grad()
        amp = ground_state_amplitude(V)
        loss = torch.sum((amp - amp_target)**2) * dx
        loss.backward()                                          # gradient w.r.t. all components of V
        return loss

    for _ in range(n_steps):
        history.append(opt.step(closure).item())                 # step() returns the loss at the START of the step
    history.append(closure().item())                             # loss after the last step
    amp = ground_state_amplitude(V.detach())
    return V.detach(), amp**2, rho_target, history

8.4 Non-trivial target densities

On the grid, a strictly positive density is the ground state of some potential: row \(i\) of the eigenvalue equation, solved for \(V_i\), gives \[(\bar H_{\rm kin}\vec\psi)_i + V_i\psi_i = E\psi_i \;\Rightarrow\; V_i = E - \frac{(\bar H_{\rm kin}\vec\psi)_i}{\psi_i}, \tag{66}\] but this potential can be very large and irregular. A density with exact zeros, such as the triangle below, cannot be reached at all. In both cases the minimisation returns the potential whose ground-state density is closest to the target in the sense of the loss. We try three targets: a triangular density, \[\rho_\ast \propto \max\big(0,\, 1 - |x|/1.5\big), \tag{67}\] with kinks and exact zeros; a flat-top density, nearly constant on \([-1.5, 1.5]\) and falling off steeply; and an asymmetric density with three peaks of heights 1, 0.6 and 0.4 (before normalisation).

The flat top, \[\rho_\ast \propto \frac{1}{1 + e^{(|x| - 1.5)/0.1}}, \tag{68}\] is a smoothed step (the Fermi function): close to 1 inside \([-1.5, 1.5]\) and close to 0 outside, with edges of width about 0.1. All three use the default n_steps=5 (about 280 loss evaluations each). With n_steps=20 the densities improve somewhat further (for the triangle the largest density error drops from about \(8\times10^{-3}\) to \(5\times10^{-3}\)), at about four times the run time. The three peaks are deliberately not too narrow: with narrower, more separated peaks the low density between them requires very high barriers, and the optimisation needs many more steps.

The three-peak density requires three wells separated by barriers; the relative heights of the peaks are set by the depths and the widths of the wells together, so the deepest well need not be under the highest peak (check this in your own run). Inside, the optimised potential oscillates from one grid point to the next. Such grid-scale structure is a sign that the target cannot be produced by any smooth potential: the optimisation uses every degree of freedom of \(V\) on the grid to approach it. Details of such optimised potentials, especially in regions of small density, can differ between computers (different linear-algebra libraries); the densities they produce do not.

This is the same scheme as training a neural network: a parametrised model (here the map from \(V\) to \(\psi_0\) through the Schrödinger equation), a loss comparing the output with a target, and gradients by automatic differentiation. Only the “model” is a physics computation instead of a network.

9. Summary

Finite differences cost \(\mathcal O(m)\) evaluations for \(m\) parameters and have limited accuracy; symbolic differentiation is exact but suffers from expression swell and cannot handle programs. Automatic differentiation gives exact derivatives of programs by applying the chain rule on the computational graph. Its forward mode (dual numbers) costs one pass per input, its reverse mode (backpropagation) one pass per output, which makes reverse mode ideal for a scalar loss of many parameters.

In PyTorch, requires_grad=True marks the variables, loss.backward() computes the gradients and .grad stores them, while torch.autograd.grad(..., create_graph=True) gives higher derivatives. Four pitfalls need care: gradients accumulate and must be reset with zero_grad(), the graph is freed after backward(), parameter updates belong inside torch.no_grad(), and .detach() must precede .numpy(). A training loop repeats zero_grad → loss → backward → step.

Automatic differentiation through eigh gives \(dE_n/d\kappa\) in agreement with the Hellmann–Feynman theorem, and in the inverse Schrödinger problem the 300 grid values of \(V\) were optimised through eigh so that \(|\psi_0|^2\) matches a target density.

Exercise 5 (advanced) — The variational principle with autograd

For the anharmonic oscillator \[V(x) = \tfrac12 x^2 + \lambda x^4,\qquad \lambda = 0.5, \tag{78}\] take the trial wavefunction \[\psi_{\alpha,\beta}(x) \propto \exp(-\alpha x^2 - \beta x^4) \tag{79}\] on the grid of Section 6, with positive \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) (parametrise them as exp of trainable numbers to keep them positive). The Rayleigh quotient \[E(\alpha,\beta) = \frac{\vec\psi^{T}\bar H\vec\psi}{\vec\psi^{T}\vec\psi} \tag{80}\] is, by the variational principle, never below the lowest eigenvalue of \(\bar H\), whatever \(\vec\psi\) is. Minimising it over \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) therefore gives the best upper bound on the ground-state energy within this family of wavefunctions. 1. Write equation (80) using the grid Hamiltonian. 2. Minimise it with Adam (for example lr=0.03, 1500 steps, starting from \(\alpha\) equal to 1 and \(\beta\) equal to \(e^{-3}\), about 0.05). Compare with the exact ground-state energy from eigvalsh, and with the best pure Gaussian, with vanishing \(\beta\). 3. Plot the exact ground state and both variational wavefunctions.

Solution

Code 
lam = 0.5
H_anh = H_kin + torch.diag(0.5 * x**2 + lam * x**4)     # grid Hamiltonian of the anharmonic oscillator
evals, evecs = torch.linalg.eigh(H_anh)                 # reference: exact diagonalisation

def variational(use_beta, steps=1500):
    """Minimise E(alpha, beta) = <psi|H|psi>/<psi|psi> for psi = exp(-alpha x^2 - beta x^4)."""
    # alpha = exp(log_a) and beta = exp(log_b) are always positive, whatever value log_a, log_b take.
    # Start: alpha = 1 (the harmonic value is 1/2) and a small quartic correction beta = e^-3 ~ 0.05. Other starts of this
    # order give the same result. With lr = 0.03 the energy stops changing after about 500 steps; 1500 steps is a safe margin.
    log_a = torch.tensor(0.0, requires_grad=True)
    log_b = torch.tensor(-3.0, requires_grad=use_beta)
    params = [log_a] + ([log_b] if use_beta else [])
    opt = torch.optim.Adam(params, lr=0.03)
    for _ in range(steps):
        opt.zero_grad()
        beta = torch.exp(log_b) if use_beta else 0.0
        psi = torch.exp(-torch.exp(log_a) * x**2 - beta * x**4)    # trial wavefunction on the grid
        # @ is matrix multiplication; for a vector psi and a matrix H, psi @ H @ psi is the number psi^T H psi.
        # The quotient (Rayleigh quotient) is never below the lowest eigenvalue: the variational principle.
        E = psi @ H_anh @ psi / (psi @ psi)
        E.backward()
        opt.step()
    return E.item(), (psi / psi.norm()).detach()

E_gb, psi_gb = variational(use_beta=True)       # Gaussian times exp(-beta x^4)

Exercise 6 (advanced, optional) — Quantum phase transition in the transverse-field Ising chain

For \(L\) spins-1/2 with periodic boundaries, \[\hat H(h) = -\sum_{i=1}^{L}\hat\sigma^z_i\hat\sigma^z_{i+1} - h\sum_{i=1}^{L}\hat\sigma^x_i . \tag{81}\] In the thermodynamic limit there is a quantum phase transition, from a ferromagnet to a paramagnet, at the critical field \(h_c\), equal to 1. 1. An operator acting on site \(i\) of the chain is \(\mathbb 1\otimes\dots\otimes\hat\sigma\otimes\dots\otimes\mathbb 1\), with \(\hat\sigma\) at position \(i\). 2. With autograd compute \[e_0'(h) = \frac{d}{dh}\frac{E_0}{L} \tag{82}\] for fields from 0.2 to 2. Verify it against the Hellmann–Feynman theorem, \[e_0'(h) = -\frac{1}{L}\langle\psi_0|\sum_i\hat\sigma^x_i|\psi_0\rangle = -m_x , \tag{83}\] minus the transverse magnetisation per site. 3. Compute \(e_0''(h)\) by central finite differences of the autograd derivative and plot it. Where is its extremum? Repeat for eight spins. What happens as \(L\) grows? (Ten spins also work but are much slower: the matrix is \(1024\times1024\) and one diagonalisation takes about 30 times longer than for eight.) 4. # Pauli matrices and the 2x2 identity
sx = torch.tensor([[0., 1.], [1., 0.]])
sz = torch.tensor([[1., 0.], [0., -1.]])
id2 = torch.eye(2)

def site_op(op, i, L):
    """op acting on site i of an L-site chain: id x ... x op x ... x id (Kronecker products)."""
    out = torch.ones(1, 1)
    for j in range(L):
        out = torch.kron(out, op if j == i else id2)
    return out

    ZZ = sum(site_op(sz, i, L) @ site_op(sz, (i + 1) % L, L) for i in range(L))
    X = sum(site_op(sx, i, L) for i in range(L))

    # 2) e0'(h) by autograd, and the Hellmann-Feynman value for comparison
