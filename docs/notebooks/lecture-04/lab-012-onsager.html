Lab 012 — Onsager’s exact solution

In Lab 010 and Lab 011 a convolutional network located the critical temperature of the two-dimensional (2D) Ising model from raw spin images. In the lecture we asked where the exact critical temperature \[T_c=\frac{2}{\ln\big(1+\sqrt2\big)}\approx2.2692 \tag{1}\] comes from. Each result of the slides Onsager’s Exact Solution is reproduced below by a computation.

We begin with what it means to “solve” the model: \(\ln Z\) of a finite lattice computed by brute force, which turns out to be always smooth. The one-dimensional chain then introduces the transfer matrix, numerically and exactly. In two dimensions the transfer matrix has size \(2^L\times2^L\) for a strip of width \(L\); with it we watch the free energy converge to Onsager’s, the specific-heat peak grow and move towards \(T_c\), and the correlation length detect \(T_c\). The high- and low-temperature expansions of \(Z\) lead to Kramers–Wannier duality and to the self-dual coupling \(K_c\). From Onsager’s formula we obtain \(f\), \(u\), \(c\) and Yang’s magnetisation \(M\), and we derive the energy per spin at \(T_c\), \(-\sqrt2\,J\), both numerically and from duality alone. The last part measures the critical exponents from the exact formulas and from the transfer matrix, compares mean-field theory with the exact solution, and tests universality on the triangular lattice.

We use units in which \(J\) and \(k_B\) are one, with no external field unless stated otherwise. The dimensionless coupling is \[K=\frac{J}{T}=\beta J. \tag{2}\]

1. The free energy and its derivatives

To “solve” the model means to know the free energy per spin, because everything else follows from it: \[ f(T) = -T\lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\ln Z, \tag{3} \] \[ Z=\sum_{\{s_i\}}e^{-H(\{s_i\})/T}, \tag{4} \] \[ H = -\sum_{\langle i,j\rangle}s_is_j . \tag{5} \] Observables are derivatives with respect to the inverse temperature \(\beta\): \[ u = \frac{\partial(\beta f)}{\partial\beta} = \frac{\langle H\rangle}{N}, \tag{6} \] \[ c = \frac{\partial u}{\partial T} = \frac{\langle H^2\rangle-\langle H\rangle^2}{NT^2}. \tag{7} \]

1.1 Brute force on a tiny lattice

On an \(L\times L\) torus there are \(2^{L^2}\) configurations. For the \(4\times4\) lattice that is 65 536, which is easy. We store every configuration as a row of \(\pm1\): the binary digits of an integer \(n\) from 0 to \(2^N-1\) give the spins, a digit 0 being read as the spin \(+1\) and a digit 1 as \(-1\).

Each configuration has the energy \[E = -2N + 2b, \tag{8}\] where \(b\) is the number of broken bonds (antiparallel neighbours): the torus has two bonds per site, and each contributes \(-1\) if satisfied and \(+1\) if broken. So \(Z\) is determined by the histogram \(n_b\) (the density of states): \[ Z(T) = \sum_{b} n_b\, e^{-(-2N+2b)/T}. \tag{9} \]

Code 
def all_configs(n):
    # all 2^n configurations of n spins, as rows of +-1 (bit k of the row index -> spin k)
    bits = (np.arange(2**n)[:, None] >> np.arange(n)) & 1
    return (1 - 2 * bits).astype(np.int8)

def torus_energies(L):
    # energies of all 2^(L*L) configurations of the L x L periodic square lattice (J = 1)
    S = all_configs(L * L).reshape(-1, L, L).astype(np.int32)
    return -(S * np.roll(S, 1, axis=1)).sum(axis=(1, 2)) - (S * np.roll(S, 1, axis=2)).sum(axis=(1, 2))

1.2 Thermodynamics of the finite lattice

With \(n_b\) in hand, \(\ln Z\), \(u\) and \(c\) are smooth functions of \(T\): \[ \ln Z = \ln\sum_b n_b e^{(2N-2b)/T}, \tag{10} \] \[ u = \frac{\sum_b n_b E_b e^{-E_b/T}}{NZ}, \tag{11} \] \[ c = \frac{\langle E^2\rangle - \langle E\rangle^2}{NT^2}. \tag{12} \] \(Z\) is a finite sum of exponentials, positive for every real \(T\), so \(\ln Z\) is analytic: a finite lattice has no phase transition. A singularity can only appear in the limit of infinitely many spins.

Code 
def thermo_from_dos(nb, N, T):
    # ln Z / N, u and c per spin from the histogram n_b of broken bonds
    T = np.atleast_1d(T)[:, None]
    Eb = -2 * N + 2 * np.arange(len(nb))
    logw = np.log(np.where(nb > 0, nb, 1)) - Eb / T
    logw = np.where(nb > 0, logw, -np.inf)
    shift = logw.max(axis=1, keepdims=True)                  # avoid overflow
    w = np.exp(logw - shift); Zr = w.sum(axis=1, keepdims=True); p = w / Zr
    E1 = (p * Eb).sum(1); E2 = (p * Eb**2).sum(1)
    lnZ = (np.log(Zr) + shift)[:, 0]
    return lnZ / N, E1 / N, (E2 - E1**2) / (N * T[:, 0]**2)

2. The 1D chain and the transfer matrix

The one-dimensional (1D) chain shows the transfer-matrix idea in its simplest form. Take \[H = -\sum_i s_is_{i+1} - h\sum_i s_i \tag{13}\] on a ring of \(N\) spins. With \(J\) set to 1 the coupling \(K\) of equation (2) is \(\beta\) itself, and we write the reduced field as \[\tilde h = \beta h. \tag{14}\] The Boltzmann weight is a product of bond factors \[ e^{-\beta H} = \prod_{i=1}^N \mathsf T_{s_is_{i+1}}, \tag{15} \] \[ \mathsf T_{ss'} = e^{Kss' + \frac{\tilde h}{2}(s+s')}, \tag{16} \] \[ \mathsf T = \begin{pmatrix} e^{K+\tilde h} & e^{-K}\\ e^{-K} & e^{K-\tilde h}\end{pmatrix}. \tag{17} \] The sum over all \(2^N\) configurations is a matrix product, closed into a trace by the periodic boundary: \[ Z = \sum_{s_1,\dots,s_N}\mathsf T_{s_1s_2}\mathsf T_{s_2s_3}\cdots\mathsf T_{s_Ns_1} = \operatorname{Tr}\mathsf T^N = \lambda_+^N + \lambda_-^N . \tag{18} \] The eigenvalues of a symmetric \(2\times2\) matrix with diagonal entries \(a\), \(d\) and off-diagonal entry \(b\) are \[\lambda=\frac{a+d}{2}\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{a-d}{2}\right)^2+b^2}, \tag{19}\] which for the matrix (17) gives \[ \lambda_\pm = e^{K}\cosh\tilde h \pm \sqrt{e^{2K}\sinh^2\tilde h + e^{-2K}}, \tag{20} \] and in zero field \[\lambda_+ = 2\cosh K,\qquad \lambda_- = 2\sinh K . \tag{21}\] The trace in equation (18) is compared with the brute-force sum, and equation (20) with the eigenvalues returned by numpy.

def Z_chain_brute(N, K, ht):
    S = all_configs(N).astype(float)
    E_over_T = -K * (S * np.roll(S, -1, axis=1)).sum(1) - ht * S.sum(1)     # beta*H
    return np.exp(-E_over_T).sum()

def T_chain(K, ht):
    return np.array([[np.exp(K + ht), np.exp(-K)], [np.exp(-K), np.exp(K - ht)]])

2.1 Free energy and correlations of the infinite chain

For a long chain \[ \ln Z = N\ln\lambda_+ + \ln\Big(1 + \big(\lambda_-/\lambda_+\big)^N\Big)\;\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\;N\ln\lambda_+ , \tag{22} \] so \[ f = -T\ln\big(2\cosh(1/T)\big), \tag{23} \] smooth at every positive temperature: there is no phase transition in 1D (Ising, 1925). The spin–spin correlation is also a matrix product, with the diagonal matrix \(\sigma^z\) whose entries are 1 and \(-1\): \[ \langle s_0s_r\rangle = \frac{\operatorname{Tr}\big(\sigma^z\mathsf T^r\sigma^z\mathsf T^{N-r}\big)}{\operatorname{Tr}\mathsf T^N} \;\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\; \Big(\frac{\lambda_-}{\lambda_+}\Big)^r = (\tanh K)^r = e^{-r/\xi}, \tag{24} \] \[ \xi = -\frac{1}{\ln\tanh K}. \tag{25} \] \(\xi\) is finite at every positive temperature and diverges only at zero temperature.

The 1D chain teaches the lesson we carry into two dimensions: for a long system only the largest eigenvalue matters, \[\ln Z \approx N\ln\lambda_{\max}, \tag{26}\] and the correlation length is set by the ratio of the two largest eigenvalues, \[\xi = \frac{1}{\ln(\lambda_{\max}/\lambda_2)}. \tag{27}\]

3. The 2D transfer matrix

Take a strip of width \(L\) (periodic in the vertical direction) and of a length \(L'\) that grows without bound. One column \(\sigma\), the spins \(s_1,\dots,s_L\), has \(2^L\) states. Bonds inside a column and between neighbouring columns give \[ Z = \operatorname{Tr}\mathsf V^{L'}, \tag{28} \] \[ \mathsf V_{\sigma\sigma'} = \exp\Big[K\sum_{i=1}^L s_is_i' + \frac K2\sum_{i=1}^L\big(s_is_{i+1} + s'_is'_{i+1}\big)\Big], \tag{29} \] where the vertical bonds are split half–half between the two columns so that \(\mathsf V\) is symmetric. Then \[ f_L(T) = -\frac{T}{L}\ln\lambda_0(L), \tag{30} \] \[ \xi_L = \frac{1}{\ln(\lambda_0/\lambda_1)}, \tag{31} \] with \(\lambda_0\) and \(\lambda_1\) the largest and the second-largest eigenvalue. Onsager’s free energy is the limit \[f = \lim_{L\to\infty}f_L . \tag{32}\]

Symmetry halves the work. Reversing all spins of a column does not change \(\mathsf V\), and in our bit encoding it maps the index \(n\) to \[\bar n = 2^L-1-n. \tag{33}\] Pairing each state with its reversed partner splits \(\mathsf V\) into an even and an odd block, \[\mathsf V^{\pm}_{nm}=\mathsf V_{nm}\pm\mathsf V_{n\bar m}, \tag{34}\] each of size \(2^{L-1}\). \(\lambda_0\) is the top of the even block (Perron–Frobenius: all entries are positive), and \(\lambda_1\) the top of the odd block, the eigenvalue that describes how a single spin’s orientation is remembered along the strip.

The construction is first tested on a known case: on an \(L\times L'\) torus equation (28) is exact, so for \(4\times4\) it must agree with the brute-force histogram of Section 1.

Code 
def transfer_matrix(L, K, lattice="square"):
    # V[sigma, sigma'] for a periodic column of L spins; 'triangular' adds the diagonal bonds s_i s'_{i+1} (Section 10)
    S = all_configs(L).astype(float)
    intra = (S * np.roll(S, -1, axis=1)).sum(1)                 # sum_i s_i s_{i+1} inside a column
    inter = S @ S.T                                             # sum_i s_i s'_i
    if lattice == "triangular":
        inter = inter + S @ np.roll(S, -1, axis=1).T            # sum_i s_i s'_{i+1}
    return np.exp(K * (inter + 0.5 * (intra[:, None] + intra[None, :])))

def top_eigenvalue(A, symmetric):
    n = A.shape[0]
    if symmetric and n <= 64:
        return eigh(A, eigvals_only=True, subset_by_index=[n - 1, n - 1])[0]
    if symmetric:
        return eigsh(A, k=1, which="LA", tol=0)[0][0]           # Lanczos: only the top eigenvalue
    if n <= 64:
        return np.max(np.linalg.eigvals(A).real)
    return eigs(A, k=1, which="LR", tol=1e-14)[0][0].real

def lambda01(L, K, lattice="square"):
    # (lambda_0, lambda_1): largest eigenvalues in the spin-flip even and odd sectors
    V = transfer_matrix(L, K, lattice)
    n = V.shape[0] // 2
    Vflip = V[:, ::-1]                                          # column m -> its flipped partner 2^L-1-m
    sym = lattice == "square"
    return (top_eigenvalue(V[:n, :n] + Vflip[:n, :n], sym),
            top_eigenvalue(V[:n, :n] - Vflip[:n, :n], sym))

3.1 Free energy and specific heat of strips

For the widths 4 to 10 we compute \[c_L = -T\,\frac{\partial^2 f_L}{\partial T^2} \tag{35}\] by finite differences on a fine grid; the eigenvalues are accurate to machine precision, so the second difference is reliable. Onsager’s \(f\) (Section 6) is plotted for comparison.

Code 
def f_onsager(T):
    # Onsager 1944: -beta f = ln(2 cosh 2K) + (1/2pi) int_0^pi ln[(1 + sqrt(1 - kappa^2 sin^2 phi))/2] dphi
    K = 1.0 / T
    k = 2 * np.sinh(2 * K) / np.cosh(2 * K)**2
    g = lambda p: np.log(0.5 * (1 + np.sqrt(max(0.0, 1 - k**2 * np.sin(p)**2))))

4. High- and low-temperature expansions of \(Z\)

At high temperature the natural small parameter is \(\tanh K\). Since the product \(ss'\) of two spins is \(\pm1\), \[e^{Kss'} = \cosh K\,(1 + t\,ss'),\qquad t = \tanh K. \tag{36}\] Expanding the product over all bonds gives a sum over subsets \(G\) of bonds; summing over each spin kills every term in which some site touches an odd number of bonds of \(G\). The survivors are closed graphs: \[ Z = 2^N(\cosh K)^{2N}\sum_{\text{closed } G}t^{|G|} = 2^N(\cosh K)^{2N}\big(1 + N t^4 + 2N t^6 + \dots\big), \tag{37} \] where the smallest closed graph is an elementary square (4 bonds, \(N\) of them), and the next a \(1\times2\) rectangle (6 bonds, \(2N\) of them: horizontal and vertical).

At low temperature we expand around order instead. Start from all spins up, with energy \(-2N\). Every broken bond costs the energy 2, that is a factor \[x = e^{-2K}, \tag{38}\] and broken bonds form closed walls on the dual lattice: \[ Z = 2e^{2NK}\big(1 + N x^4 + 2N x^6 + \dots\big). \tag{39} \] One flipped spin breaks 4 bonds (\(N\) ways), a flipped neighbouring pair breaks 6 bonds (\(2N\) ways), and the factor 2 is the all-down copy.

The counting can be checked directly on the \(4\times4\) torus: half the histogram \(n_b\) of Section 1 should be 16, the number of sites, for four broken bonds and 32 for six.

5. Kramers–Wannier duality and the self-dual point

Call \[g(y) = \lim_{N\to\infty}\frac1N\ln\sum_G y^{|G|} . \tag{43}\] The two expansions, equations (37) and (39), say \[ \varphi(K) \equiv \frac{\ln Z(K)}{N} = \ln 2 + 2\ln\cosh K + g(\tanh K) = 2K + g\big(e^{-2K}\big). \tag{44} \] Define the dual coupling \(K^*\) by \[e^{-2K^*} = \tanh K . \tag{45}\] Using the second form of equation (44) at \(K^*\) and the first at \(K\), both contain \(g(\tanh K)\), so \[ \boxed{\ \varphi(K) - \ln2 - 2\ln\cosh K = \varphi(K^*) - 2K^*\ }. \tag{46} \] An equivalent form of the duality map (45) is \[\sinh2K\,\sinh2K^* = 1, \tag{47}\] which follows from \[\sinh 2K^* = \frac{1-\tanh^2K}{2\tanh K} = \frac{1}{\sinh2K}. \tag{48}\] Small \(K\) (hot) maps to large \(K^*\) (cold). If there is a single singularity, it must sit at the self-dual point, the coupling \(K_c\) that is its own dual: \[ \sinh 2K_c = 1\ \Rightarrow\ e^{2K_c} = 1+\sqrt2\ \Rightarrow\ T_c = \frac{2}{\ln(1+\sqrt2)}, \tag{49} \] which is equation (1). We check: (i) equation (47), (ii) Onsager’s modulus \(\kappa\) of equation (51) is the same at \(K\) and \(K^*\), (iii) the identity (46) holds for Onsager’s \(f\), (iv) the fixed point of the duality map, found by root finding, is \(K_c\).

6. Onsager’s formula

\[ -\beta f = \ln(2\cosh2K) + \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^\pi d\phi\,\ln\Big[\tfrac12\Big(1+\sqrt{1-\kappa^2\sin^2\phi}\Big)\Big], \tag{50} \] \[ \kappa = \frac{2\sinh2K}{\cosh^22K} = \frac{2s}{1+s^2},\qquad s = \sinh 2K . \tag{51} \] Since \[2s\le1+s^2, \tag{52}\] \(\kappa\) never exceeds 1, and it reaches 1 only where \(s\) is 1, that is at \(T_c\) by equation (49). There the square root vanishes at the angle \(\pi/2\) and the integrand develops a kink: this is the origin of the non-analyticity.

The energy per spin, equation (6), can be computed in two independent ways, and we use both. The first is numerical: differentiate the integral formula by finite differences in \(\beta\). The second uses the complete elliptic integral of the first kind, \[K_1(\kappa) = \int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\phi}{\sqrt{1-\kappa^2\sin^2\phi}}, \tag{53}\] in the formula \[ u = -\coth2K\Big[1 + \frac2\pi\big(2\tanh^22K - 1\big)K_1(\kappa)\Big]. \tag{54} \] scipy.special.ellipkm1(p) returns \(K_1\) as a function of the complementary parameter \[p = 1-\kappa^2 = \Big(\frac{1-s^2}{1+s^2}\Big)^2 . \tag{55}\] Near \(T_c\), \(\kappa^2\) is so close to 1 that \(p\) must be computed from the right-hand form, without cancellation. Yang’s spontaneous magnetisation is \[M = \begin{cases}\big[1-\sinh^{-4}2K\big]^{1/8}, & T<T_c,\\[2pt] 0, & T>T_c.\end{cases} \tag{56}\]

def u_elliptic(T):
    K = 1.0 / np.asarray(T, dtype=float)
    s = np.sinh(2 * K)
    p = ((1 - s**2) / (1 + s**2))**2                 # 1 - kappa^2, without cancellation
    return -1 / np.tanh(2 * K) * (1 + 2 / np.pi * (2 * np.tanh(2 * K)**2 - 1) * ellipkm1(p))

7.2 The value from duality

The same number follows from the duality of Section 5 alone, without Onsager’s formula. Differentiate the duality identity (46) with respect to \(K\). Differentiating equation (46) gives \[ \varepsilon(K) - 2\tanh K = \big(\varepsilon(K^*) - 2\big)\frac{dK^*}{dK}. \tag{60} \] Differentiating equation (45), \[ -2e^{-2K^*}dK^* = \frac{dK}{\cosh^2K}\ \Rightarrow\ \frac{dK^*}{dK} = -\frac{1}{2\tanh K\cosh^2K} = -\frac{1}{\sinh 2K}, \tag{61} \] which is \(-1\) at \(K_c\) by equation (49). At the self-dual point \(K\) and \(K^*\) both coincide with \(K_c\), and if \(u\) is continuous there (no latent heat), equation (60) becomes \[ \varepsilon - 2\tanh K_c = -(\varepsilon - 2)\ \Rightarrow\ \varepsilon(K_c) = 1 + \tanh K_c . \tag{62} \] Finally, by self-duality, \[\tanh K_c = e^{-2K_c} = \frac{1}{1+\sqrt2} = \sqrt2 - 1, \tag{63}\] so that \[\varepsilon(K_c) = \sqrt2,\qquad u(T_c) = -\sqrt2 . \tag{64}\] Two exact results, \(T_c\) and \(u(T_c)\), thus come from a symmetry argument; each step of the derivation is evaluated numerically below.

8. Critical exponents

Near \(T_c\) the exact formulas behave as powers (or logarithms) of the reduced temperature \[t = \frac{T-T_c}{T_c}, \tag{65}\] and the exponents of these laws can be read off numerically.

For the magnetisation, \[M\sim(-t)^\beta, \tag{66}\] we fit \(\ln M\) against \(\ln(-t)\) close to \(T_c\), and we also look at the local slope \(d\ln M/d\ln(-t)\), which must tend to \(\beta\).

The specific heat diverges logarithmically, \[c\sim -A\ln|t|, \tag{67}\] which corresponds to a vanishing exponent \(\alpha\). We fit \[c = -A\ln|t| + B_\pm \tag{68}\] on both sides of \(T_c\) and compare the fitted amplitude with the exact value \[A = \frac{2}{\pi}\Big(\frac{2}{T_c}\Big)^2 . \tag{69}\] Here \(c\) is obtained from the elliptic \(u\) by a central difference with a step much smaller than \(|T-T_c|\).

8.1 Exponents \(\eta\) and \(\nu\) from the transfer matrix

The strips of Section 3 give two more exponents, independently of Onsager’s formula.

The exponent \(\eta\) follows from the correlation length at \(T_c\), which on a strip is proportional to the width. Conformal invariance of the critical point fixes the constant on a periodic strip, \[\xi_L = \frac{L}{\pi\eta}, \tag{70}\] so \[\eta_L = \frac{L}{\pi\,\xi_L(T_c)} \tag{71}\] should tend to \(\eta\), whose exact value is 1/4.

The exponent \(\nu\) follows from finite-size scaling. Near \(T_c\), \(\xi_L/L\) is a function of \(L^{1/\nu}t\) only, so its slope at \(T_c\) grows as \[\frac{d(\xi_L/L)}{dT}\Big|_{T_c}\propto L^{1/\nu}. \tag{72}\] The exact value of \(\nu\) is 1.

As a by-product we test the convergence of the free energy at \(T_c\), \[f_L - f = -\frac{\pi\,c_{\rm CFT}\,T_c}{6L^2}, \tag{73}\] where the central charge \(c_{\rm CFT}\) of conformal field theory (CFT) for the Ising universality class is one half; in other words \[L^2(f_L - f)\to-\frac{\pi T_c}{12}. \tag{74}\]

9. Mean-field theory

Mean-field (MF) theory replaces the neighbours of each spin by their average \(m\): each spin feels the field \(zm\), where \(z\), the number of neighbours, is 4, so \[ m = \tanh\Big(\frac{4m}{T}\Big). \tag{76} \] Zero magnetisation is always a solution; a non-zero one exists when the slope of the right-hand side at the origin exceeds 1, that is below \[T_c^{\rm MF} = 4 . \tag{77}\] Near \(T_c^{\rm MF}\) the expansion \[\tanh x\approx x - \frac{x^3}{3} \tag{78}\] gives \[m \approx \frac4Tm - \frac13\Big(\frac4T\Big)^3m^3,\qquad m^2\approx \frac{3\big(\frac4T-1\big)}{\big(\frac4T\big)^3}, \tag{79}\] so \[m\sim\big(1 - T/T_c^{\rm MF}\big)^{1/2},\qquad \beta_{\rm MF} = \tfrac12 . \tag{80}\] The mean-field energy per spin, \[u_{\rm MF} = -\frac z2m^2 = -2m^2, \tag{81}\] vanishes above \(T_c^{\rm MF}\) and has a finite slope below, which gives a jump in \(c\) instead of a divergence. \(T_c^{\rm MF}\), \(\beta_{\rm MF}\) and the specific heat just below \(T_c^{\rm MF}\) are also found numerically, and the mean-field magnetisation is plotted together with Yang’s exact \(M\).

10. The triangular lattice and universality

Add one diagonal bond to every plaquette: each spin now has six neighbours. In the transfer matrix this adds the term \[K\sum_is_is'_{i+1} \tag{82}\] to the exponent of equation (29) (flag lattice="triangular"; \(\mathsf V\) is no longer symmetric, so we use a general eigensolver). The exact results are the critical temperature \[T_c^{\triangle} = \frac{4}{\ln3} \tag{83}\] and the same exponents as on the square lattice. We test both with the crossing method of Section 3 and with \(\eta_L\).

One geometric correction is needed. In the triangular lattice the columns are \(\sqrt3/2\) apart (the sites of one column are at distance 1), so the physical correlation length is \(\xi_L\) multiplied by \(\sqrt3/2\), and \[\eta_L = \frac{L}{\pi\,\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\,\xi_L}. \tag{84}\]
