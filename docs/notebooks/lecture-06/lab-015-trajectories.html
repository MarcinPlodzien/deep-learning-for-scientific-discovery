Physical parameters from trajectories

Deep Learning for Scientific Discovery, Jagiellonian University. Author: Marcin Płodzień.

A mass on a spring that moves in a fluid is summarised by a physicist with a few numbers. They are the frequency of the oscillation, the rate at which it dies out and the initial position and velocity. We give a neural network only the recorded positions of many such oscillators, each with its own spring, its own friction and its own initial condition, and all of them shaken by a weak random force. One sample \(\mathbf x_i\) is the trajectory of one oscillator, its positions at 100 equally spaced times. The network is the β-variational autoencoder (β-VAE) of Lecture 6. It compresses each trajectory into a few latent coordinates, and the weight \(\beta\) pushes it to use these coordinates sparingly and independently. Because we simulate the trajectories, we know the true physical parameters of every one of them, and we can check which of these parameters the latent coordinates have learned. The same question was asked by Iten et al., Phys. Rev. Lett. 124, 010508 (2020) for the spring constant and the damping of a pendulum.

We start with the physics of the damped oscillator driven by Gaussian white noise and with its numerical integration, which we test against exact results. We then introduce the data set, train the β-VAE for several values of \(\beta\) and several random seeds, and read its latent coordinates with the tools of Lecture 6. These are the active coordinates, correlations, the mutual information gap and latent traversals. An analysis of how sensitive a trajectory is to each parameter explains what the network finds. We then reimplement the question-and-answer network of Iten et al. and compare it with the β-VAE on their pendulum data. In the last part we repeat the experiment for two coupled masses, whose motion depends on the springs only through two normal-mode frequencies. All networks are small and train on a CPU, and their trained weights are stored in results/ and loaded when present.

The noisy damped oscillator

Langevin equation

A particle of mass \(m\) is attached to a spring of spring constant \(k\) and moves in a viscous fluid. The spring pulls it back with a force proportional to the displacement, the fluid slows it down with a friction force proportional to the velocity, and the molecules of the fluid hit it at random. Dividing Newton’s equation by \(m\) we obtain the Langevin equation of the damped oscillator, \[ \ddot x=-\omega^2x-\gamma\dot x+\sqrt{2D}\,\xi(t), \tag{1} \] with the angular frequency of the oscillator without friction \[ \omega=\sqrt{k/m}. \tag{2} \] Equation (1) is a stochastic differential equation (SDE), a differential equation with a random term. Its solution is a random function of time, and every realisation of the random force gives a different trajectory. The friction rate \(\gamma\) is the inverse of the time in which friction alone would stop a free particle. The random force \(\xi(t)\) is Gaussian white noise, a Gaussian random function with \[ \langle\xi(t)\rangle=0,\qquad\langle\xi(t)\,\xi(t')\rangle=\delta(t-t'), \tag{3} \] so its values at different times are independent, however close the times are. The brackets \(\langle\cdot\rangle\) denote the average over many realisations of the noise. The constant \(D\) is the noise strength and sets the size of the random force. Since \(\xi\) has the unit \(\mathrm{s}^{-1/2}\), the noise strength has the unit \(\mathrm{m}^2/\mathrm{s}^3\). Equation (19) will show that in the stationary state the mean squared velocity is the ratio of \(D\) and \(\gamma\). If the random force comes from a fluid at temperature \(T\), equipartition fixes the mean squared velocity at \(k_BT/m\), with the Boltzmann constant \(k_B\), and therefore \[ D=\frac{\gamma\,k_BT}{m}. \tag{4} \] Friction and noise thus have the same molecular origin and are tied together by this Einstein relation. All quantities in the notebook are dimensionless, in units in which the mass equals one.

Without noise the equation is linear. The exponential ansatz \(e^{\lambda t}\) for \(x(t)\) gives the characteristic equation \[ \lambda^2+\gamma\lambda+\omega^2=0 \tag{5} \] with the roots \[ \lambda_\pm=-\frac\gamma2\pm\sqrt{\frac{\gamma^2}{4}-\omega^2}. \tag{6} \] Two regimes follow from the sign of the expression under the root. In the underdamped regime, \[ \gamma<2\omega, \tag{7} \] the roots are complex, and the particle oscillates at the damped frequency \[ \omega_d=\sqrt{\omega^2-\gamma^2/4} \tag{8} \] inside an exponentially decaying envelope, \[ x(t)=e^{-\gamma t/2}\Big[x_0\cos\omega_dt+\frac{v_0+\gamma x_0/2}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\Big], \tag{9} \] where \(x_0\) and \(v_0\) are the initial position and velocity. The frequency \(\omega\) sets how often the particle crosses zero, and the friction rate \(\gamma\) sets how fast the oscillation dies out. In the overdamped regime, when \(\gamma\) exceeds \(2\omega\), both roots are real and negative. The particle then creeps back to the origin without oscillating, and the slower of the two rates dominates at long times. At the boundary between the two regimes, called critical damping, the return to the origin is fastest. We check the roots and the underdamped solution (9) with SymPy and expand the slow rate for strong friction.

The residual vanishes and the initial conditions are reproduced. For strong friction the slow rate is \[ |\lambda_+|\approx\frac{\omega^2}{\gamma}+\frac{\omega^4}{\gamma^3}. \tag{10} \] Deep in the overdamped regime a trajectory therefore depends on \(\omega\) and \(\gamma\) essentially only through the combination \(\omega^2/\gamma\), and we will test the latent coordinates against this prediction.

For any regime we can write the noise-free motion compactly with the state vector \(\mathbf s\), which collects the position and the velocity, \[ \dot{\mathbf s}=A\mathbf s,\qquad A=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-\omega^2&-\gamma\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf s(t)=e^{At}\,\mathbf s(0), \tag{11} \] where \(A\) is the drift matrix. The function mean_trajectory evaluates the matrix exponential with torch.linalg.matrix_exp. In the section on the exact mean we show that the same function gives the mean of the noisy trajectories, so it serves as the exact reference throughout. Since it is differentiable, it also provides the derivatives of a trajectory with respect to the parameters, which we need for the sensitivity analysis.

Code 
def mean_trajectory(p, t):
    """Noise-free trajectory: first component of expm(A t) (x0, v0); p = (omega, gamma, x0, v0) and t (T,), float64 tensors."""
    omega, gamma, x0, v0 = p
    zero, one = torch.zeros_like(omega), torch.ones_like(omega)
    A = torch.stack([torch.stack([zero, one]), torch.stack([-omega**2, -gamma])])        # drift matrix
    return (torch.linalg.matrix_exp(A * t[:, None, None]) @ torch.stack([x0, v0]))[:, 0]

def mean_trajectories(F, t):
    """Many trajectories at once: F (N, 4) = (omega, gamma, x0, v0), times t (T,) -> numpy array (N, T)."""
    return vmap(mean_trajectory, in_dims=(0, None))(torch.as_tensor(np.asarray(F, float), dtype=torch.float64),
                                                    torch.as_tensor(np.asarray(t, float), dtype=torch.float64)).numpy()

Euler–Maruyama integration

To simulate equation (1) we write it as two first-order equations, for \(x\) and for the velocity \(v\), and integrate them over one small time step \(\Delta t\). The integral of \(\xi\) over one step is the increment \(\Delta W\) of the Wiener process, the mathematical model of Brownian motion. It is Gaussian with mean zero, and by equation (3) its variance equals the step, \[ \Delta W=\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\xi(s)\,ds,\qquad\langle\Delta W^2\rangle=\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\!\!\int_t^{t+\Delta t}\langle\xi(s)\xi(s')\rangle\,ds\,ds'=\Delta t. \tag{12} \] We can therefore write it as \[ \Delta W=\sqrt{\Delta t}\,\eta,\qquad\eta\sim\mathcal N(0,1), \tag{13} \] and increments over different steps are independent. Approximating the smooth terms by their values at the beginning of the step we obtain the update \[ \begin{aligned} x_{n+1}&=x_n+v_n\,\Delta t,\\ v_{n+1}&=v_n+\big(-\omega^2x_n-\gamma v_n\big)\Delta t+\sqrt{2D\,\Delta t}\;\eta_n, \end{aligned} \tag{14} \] with independent standard Gaussian numbers \(\eta_n\). This is the Euler–Maruyama scheme [Kloeden and Platen 1992, Higham 2001], the stochastic counterpart of the Euler method for ordinary differential equations. It differs from the ordinary Euler method by the factor \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\) in the noise term. The random kicks of many steps add up like a random walk, so their typical size grows as the square root of the elapsed time (Exercise 1). Averages computed with the scheme converge to the exact ones with an error proportional to \(\Delta t\), which is called weak order one. We use \(\Delta t=0.01\), small compared with the shortest time scales of the data, the inverse frequency (at least 0.5) and the inverse friction rate (at least 1/6).

The function simulate integrates many oscillators at once, vectorised over the trajectories with NumPy, and records every n_sub-th step.

Code 
DT = 0.01            # Euler-Maruyama time step
D_NOISE = 0.002      # noise strength D of all data sets

def simulate(omega, gamma, x0, v0, rng, D=D_NOISE, dt=DT, n_sub=6, n_rec=100, drive=None):
    """Euler-Maruyama for N independent oscillators; omega, gamma, x0, v0 are arrays of shape (N,).
    drive: optional function of the time returning an additional force (N,).
    Returns positions and velocities (N, n_rec) at the times 0, n_sub*dt, 2*n_sub*dt, ..."""
    x, v = np.array(x0, float), np.array(v0, float)
    X, V = np.empty((len(x), n_rec)), np.empty((len(x), n_rec))
    X[:, 0], V[:, 0] = x, v
    kick = np.sqrt(2 * D * dt)                                     # the noise term scales as sqrt(dt)
    n = 0
    for i in range(1, n_rec):
        for _ in range(n_sub):                                     # n_sub steps between two recorded points
            a = -omega**2 * x - gamma * v + (drive(n * dt) if drive is not None else 0.0)
            x, v = x + v * dt, v + a * dt + kick * rng.standard_normal(len(x))
            n += 1
        X[:, i], V[:, i] = x, v
    return X, V

Exact mean and stationary variance

Averaging the update (14) over the noise removes the term with \(\eta_n\), because its mean vanishes and the update is linear. The averages of \(x_n\) and \(v_n\) therefore obey the noise-free recursion, and for vanishing \(\Delta t\) the mean trajectory is the noise-free solution (11).

For the fluctuations we write the update as \[ \mathbf s_{n+1}=M\mathbf s_n+\mathbf b\,\eta_n,\qquad M=I+A\,\Delta t,\qquad\mathbf b=\begin{pmatrix}0\\\sqrt{2D\,\Delta t}\end{pmatrix}. \tag{15} \] Since \(\eta_n\) is independent of \(\mathbf s_n\), the covariance matrix \[ \Sigma=\langle\mathbf s\mathbf s^{\mathsf T}\rangle-\langle\mathbf s\rangle\langle\mathbf s\rangle^{\mathsf T} \tag{16} \] obeys \[ \Sigma_{n+1}=M\Sigma_nM^{\mathsf T}+\mathbf b\mathbf b^{\mathsf T},\qquad \frac{\Sigma_{n+1}-\Sigma_n}{\Delta t}=A\Sigma_n+\Sigma_nA^{\mathsf T}+Q+O(\Delta t),\qquad Q=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&2D\end{pmatrix}. \tag{17} \] In the stationary state the left-hand side of the second relation vanishes, and the covariance solves the Lyapunov equation \[ A\Sigma+\Sigma A^{\mathsf T}+Q=0. \tag{18} \] These are three linear equations for the second moments of position and velocity, which we solve with SymPy.

The stationary second moments are \[ \langle x^2\rangle=\frac{D}{\gamma\omega^2},\qquad\langle xv\rangle=0,\qquad\langle v^2\rangle=\frac D\gamma, \tag{19} \] and the velocity variance is the one used for the Einstein relation (4). In the overdamped limit the inertia can be neglected, and the particle obeys \[ \gamma\dot x=-\omega^2x+\sqrt{2D}\,\xi(t). \tag{20} \] This is the Ornstein–Uhlenbeck process [Uhlenbeck and Ornstein 1930], a Gaussian process with a drift back to the origin whose correlations decay exponentially in time. Its stationary position variance is the same as in equation (19) (Exercise 2). For a particle that starts at a fixed point the covariance at time \(t\) is \[ \Sigma(t)=\Sigma_\infty-e^{At}\,\Sigma_\infty\,e^{A^{\mathsf T}t}, \tag{21} \] where \(\Sigma_\infty\) is the stationary solution of equation (18). This expression solves the continuum form of equation (17) and vanishes at the start.

We test the integrator against these results with 20 000 realisations at \(\omega=1.5\), \(\gamma=0.5\), \(x_0=1\) and \(v_0=0\) up to time 40. We compare the ensemble mean with equation (11) and the ensemble variance with equation (21). The Euler–Maruyama recursion itself has a stationary covariance, the solution of the first relation in equation (17) with equal \(\Sigma_{n+1}\) and \(\Sigma_n\). This discrete Lyapunov equation is solved by scipy.linalg.solve_discrete_lyapunov, and comparing its solution with equation (19) shows how large the error of order \(\Delta t\) is.

Trajectory data

We simulate the data with equation (1), so nothing is downloaded. One sample of the data set is the trajectory of one oscillator, \[ \mathbf x_i=\big(x_i(t_0),x_i(t_1),\dots,x_i(t_{99})\big)\in\mathbb R^{100},\qquad t_j=0.06\,j, \tag{22} \] the position of the mass at 100 equally spaced times that cover the window from 0 to 5.94. All quantities are dimensionless. The mass is one, times are measured in a unit in which the frequencies are of order one, and positions in the unit of a typical initial displacement. Every trajectory has its own physical parameters and its own initial condition, drawn independently from \[ \omega\sim\mathcal U[1,2],\qquad\ln\gamma\sim\mathcal U[\ln0.2,\ln6],\qquad x_0\sim\mathcal U[0.5,1.5],\qquad v_0\sim\mathcal U[-0.5,0.5], \tag{23} \] where \(\mathcal U[a,b]\) denotes the uniform distribution on the interval from \(a\) to \(b\). The friction rate is log-uniform, so every factor of \(e\) in the friction is equally represented over its range from 0.2 to 6. The noise strength \(D=0.002\) is the same for all trajectories. We integrate each trajectory with the update (14) at \(\Delta t=0.01\) and record every sixth step. The training set has 20 000 trajectories and the test set 5000, simulated with different random seeds. Both regimes of equation (7) are present.

We call the four numbers \((\omega,\gamma,x_0,v_0)\) the factors of a trajectory. Only \(\omega\) and \(\gamma\) are properties of the oscillator. The initial position and velocity are nuisance factors, which change the trajectory without describing the system. The factors serve as labels for the evaluation only, and the networks never see them.

The ranges are a deliberate choice. Over the window the oscillation completes about one to two periods, and the initial position is always positive, so a trajectory cannot be turned into another one by flipping its sign. Wider ranges, with many periods in the window or with initial positions of both signs, make the task much harder for the network, and the sensitivity analysis explains why.

β-VAE for trajectories

Gaussian likelihood and the β-VAE loss

We use the notation of Lecture 6. A trajectory \(\mathbf x\in\mathbb R^T\), with \(T=100\) positions, is described by a latent vector \(\mathbf z\in\mathbb R^d\) with the prior \(p(\mathbf z)\) and the encoder distribution \(q_\phi(\mathbf z|\mathbf x)\), \[ p(\mathbf z)=\mathcal N(0,I_d),\qquad q_\phi(\mathbf z|\mathbf x)=\mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\mu_\phi(\mathbf x),\mathrm{diag}\,\boldsymbol\sigma^2_\phi(\mathbf x)\big), \tag{24} \] and the decoder defines a probability distribution of trajectories \(p_\theta(\mathbf x|\mathbf z)\). Positions are real numbers, and the natural choice is a Gaussian likelihood. Given \(\mathbf z\), every recorded position is Gaussian around the value of a decoder network \(G_\theta(\mathbf z)\), with a fixed standard deviation \(\sigma_x\), \[ p_\theta(\mathbf x|\mathbf z)=\prod_{i=1}^T\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma_x^2}}\exp\Big[-\frac{\big(x_i-G_\theta(\mathbf z)_i\big)^2}{2\sigma_x^2}\Big],\qquad -\log p_\theta(\mathbf x|\mathbf z)=\frac{1}{2\sigma_x^2}\big\lVert\mathbf x-G_\theta(\mathbf z)\big\rVert^2+\frac T2\log(2\pi\sigma_x^2). \tag{25} \] The reconstruction term is therefore proportional to the mean squared error (MSE), the average squared deviation of the reconstruction from the data, \[ \mathrm{MSE}=\frac1T\sum_{i=1}^T\big(x_i-G_\theta(\mathbf z)_i\big)^2, \tag{26} \] multiplied by \(T/(2\sigma_x^2)\), plus a constant that does not affect training. The decoder output \(G_\theta(\mathbf z)\) is the mean of the trajectory given \(\mathbf z\), a smooth curve without the noise. The scale \(\sigma_x\) says how large a deviation the model tolerates. We set it to 0.1, about three times the typical deviation of a noisy trajectory from its mean (measured in the data section), so the model gains little by storing the noise.

The β-VAE loss of Lecture 6 is \[ \mathcal L_\beta(\mathbf x)=\underbrace{\mathbb E_{q_\phi(\mathbf z|\mathbf x)}\Big[\frac{1}{2\sigma_x^2}\big\lVert\mathbf x-G_\theta(\mathbf z)\big\rVert^2\Big]}_{\text{distortion}}+\beta\,\underbrace{\mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(\mathbf z|\mathbf x)\,\|\,p(\mathbf z)\big)}_{\text{rate}}, \tag{27} \] with the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence of the encoder distribution from the prior, \[ \mathrm{KL}\big(q_\phi(\mathbf z|\mathbf x)\,\|\,p(\mathbf z)\big)=\frac12\sum_{k=1}^d\big(\mu_k^2+\sigma_k^2-\ln\sigma_k^2-1\big). \tag{28} \] The KL divergence measures, in nats (units of information with the natural logarithm), how much the encoder distribution of one trajectory differs from the prior. It vanishes only when every coordinate has zero mean and unit variance, and it is the price of the information that the code carries about the trajectory. For \(\beta=1\) the loss is minus the evidence lower bound (ELBO), the lower bound on \(\log p(\mathbf x)\) that an ordinary VAE maximises. A larger \(\beta\) makes information more expensive and pushes the code towards fewer and more independent coordinates [Higgins et al. 2017]. Only the product of \(2\sigma_x^2\) and \(\beta\) matters for this trade-off, so doubling \(\sigma_x^2\) has the same effect as doubling \(\beta\). We estimate the expectation over \(q_\phi\) with one sample, written with the reparametrisation trick as \[ \mathbf z=\boldsymbol\mu+\boldsymbol\sigma\odot\boldsymbol\varepsilon,\qquad\boldsymbol\varepsilon\sim\mathcal N(0,I_d). \tag{29} \] After training we call the average KL over the test trajectories the rate \(R\) and the average reconstruction term the distortion. Both are measured in nats per trajectory.

Network

A trajectory is a short vector of 100 numbers, and a dense network is enough. The encoder has two hidden layers of 256 and 128 units, each followed by the rectified linear unit \[ \mathrm{ReLU}(u)=\max(0,u), \tag{30} \] and two linear heads that return \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\log\boldsymbol\sigma^2\), each of length \(d=8\). The decoder mirrors the encoder with 128 and 256 units and ReLU, followed by a linear output layer of 100 numbers, the mean trajectory \(G_\theta(\mathbf z)\). The latent dimension is twice the number of factors. The network thus has room to spare, and the coordinates it switches off tell us how many it needs. The class takes the input length as an argument, and the two coupled masses use it with 200 inputs. The function torchinfo.summary lists every layer with its output shape and its number of parameters.

SIGMA_X = 0.1        # standard deviation of the Gaussian decoder p_theta(x | z)

class TrajVAE(nn.Module):
    """Dense VAE for trajectories of n_in numbers.
    encoder: x (B, n_in) -> 256 -> 128 -> mu (B, d), logvar (B, d);  decoder: z (B, d) -> 128 -> 256 -> mean trajectory (B, n_in)"""
    def __init__(self, n_in, d, hidden=(256, 128)):
        super().__init__()
        h1, h2 = hidden
        self.enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(n_in, h1), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(h1, h2), nn.ReLU())
        self.mu, self.logvar = nn.Linear(h2, d), nn.Linear(h2, d)                       # two heads
        self.dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d, h2), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(h2, h1), nn.ReLU(), nn.Linear(h1, n_in))
    def encode(self, x):
        """q_phi(z | x) = N(mu, diag exp(logvar)): returns (mu, logvar), each (B, d)."""
        h = self.enc(x)
        return self.mu(h), self.logvar(h)
    def decode(self, z):
        """Mean trajectory G_theta(z), (B, n_in)."""
        return self.dec(z)
    def forward(self, x):
        mu, logvar = self.encode(x)
        return self.decode(reparametrise(mu, logvar)), mu, logvar

def kl_per_dim(mu, logvar):
    """KL( N(mu, sigma^2) || N(0, 1) ) for every latent coordinate (tensors or arrays); same shape as mu."""
    exp = torch.exp if torch.is_tensor(mu) else np.exp
    return 0.5 * (mu**2 + exp(logvar) - logvar - 1)

def reparametrise(mu, logvar):
    """z = mu + sigma * eps, eps ~ N(0, I)."""
    return mu + torch.exp(0.5 * logvar) * torch.randn_like(mu)

def beta_vae_loss(model, batch, beta):
    """beta-VAE loss per trajectory, averaged over the minibatch; the constant (T/2) ln(2 pi sigma_x^2) is dropped."""
    x = batch[0]
    mu, logvar = model.encode(x)
    rec = ((model.decode(reparametrise(mu, logvar)) - x) ** 2).sum(1).mean() / (2 * SIGMA_X**2)
    kl = kl_per_dim(mu, logvar).sum(1).mean()
    return rec + beta * kl, {"loss": rec + beta * kl, "rec": rec, "kl": kl}

Training

We train all models with the same function, which uses Adam (adaptive moment estimation) with learning rate \(10^{-3}\) and minibatches of 128 trajectories. Adam is a gradient method that rescales the step of every parameter by running averages of its gradient and of its squared gradient. If a file with the trained weights exists in results/, the weights are loaded and the training is skipped. Setting TRAIN = True trains every network again.

We train four values of \(\beta\), namely 0.5, 1, 4 and 8, each with three random seeds and for 80 epochs over the 20 000 training trajectories. Several seeds are needed because the outcome of unsupervised disentangling depends on the initialisation [Locatello et al. 2019]. On the 5000 test trajectories we compute the rate and the distortion with one sample \(\mathbf z\) per trajectory. As a reference we compute the reconstruction term of the exact mean trajectory, \[ \Big\langle\frac{1}{2\sigma_x^2}\big\lVert\mathbf x-\langle\mathbf x\rangle\big\rVert^2\Big\rangle, \tag{31} \] which is the distortion of a model that knows the four factors perfectly and knows nothing about the noise. We call it the noise floor.

Latent coordinates and physical parameters

We read the latent codes with the protocol of Lecture 6. We first find the coordinates that carry information, then compare them with the known factors through correlations and the mutual information gap, then decode traversals, and finally explain the outcome by the physics of the trajectories.

A coordinate whose posterior equals the prior for every trajectory carries no information. As in Lecture 6, we call a coordinate active if its average KL exceeds 0.1 nats.

Correlations

The first probe is the Pearson correlation between the latent mean \(\mu_k(\mathbf x)\) and a factor \(v\) over the test set, \[ \rho(\mu_k,v)=\frac{\langle\mu_kv\rangle-\langle\mu_k\rangle\langle v\rangle}{\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}\,\mu_k\;\mathrm{Var}\,v}}. \tag{33} \] It lies between \(-1\) and 1 and detects linear relations only. The friction enters as \(\ln\gamma\), because \(\gamma\) spans a factor of 30 and is drawn uniformly in \(\ln\gamma\). The sign of a latent coordinate is arbitrary, so we report \(|\rho|\).

Entropy, mutual information and the gap

The second probe is the mutual information gap (MIG) of Chen et al. (2018), also used in Lecture 6, which detects nonlinear relations as well. It is built from two quantities of information theory, both of them expectations of logarithms of probabilities like the KL divergence (28) of the loss.

The entropy of a factor measures how uncertain its value is before anything is observed. An outcome \(b\) of probability \(p(b)\) carries the surprise \(-\ln p(b)\), which is zero for a certain outcome and large for a rare one. The entropy is the average surprise, \[ H(v)=-\sum_b p(b)\ln p(b), \tag{34} \] measured in nats. For a variable with \(n\) equally likely values it equals \(\ln n\), and it grows with the number of values that have to be told apart. The factors of the trajectories are continuous, so we discretise each of them into 20 bins of equal population, with the bin edges at the quantiles of the factor over the test set. Every bin then holds 5% of the test trajectories, and the entropy of each discretised factor is \(\ln20\), about 3.00 nats. In this description the frequency, the damping and the two initial values are equally uncertain, whatever their physical units. The printed entropies confirm the value.

The mutual information of a latent coordinate \(z\) and a factor \(v\) measures how much of this uncertainty disappears once \(z\) is known. It is the entropy of the factor minus the conditional entropy \(H(v|z)\), the uncertainty about the factor that remains after reading the coordinate. It can also be written as the KL divergence between the joint distribution of the two variables and the product of their marginal distributions, which is the joint distribution they would have if they were independent, \[ I(z;v)=H(v)-H(v|z)=\sum_{a,b}p(a,b)\,\ln\frac{p(a,b)}{p(a)\,p(b)}=\mathrm{KL}\big(p(z,v)\,\|\,p(z)\,p(v)\big). \tag{35} \] The mutual information is zero when the coordinate and the factor are independent, and it reaches \(H(v)\) when the coordinate determines the factor completely. The correlation (33) misses relations that are not linear, and the mutual information detects them, for example a coordinate that grows with the frequency for small initial positions and falls for large ones. Since the mutual information cannot exceed the entropy of the factor, the normalised mutual information \[ \tilde I(z;v)=\frac{I(z;v)}{H(v)} \tag{36} \] lies between 0, when the coordinate says nothing about the factor, and 1, when it determines the factor. We read it as the fraction of the uncertainty about the factor that the coordinate removes.

The MIG condenses the matrix of normalised mutual informations into one number. For each factor \(v_k\) it takes the normalised mutual information of the most informative coordinate \(z_{(1)}\) minus that of the second most informative coordinate \(z_{(2)}\), and it averages these gaps over the \(K\) factors, here four, \[ \mathrm{MIG}=\frac1K\sum_{k=1}^{K}\Big[\tilde I\big(z_{(1)};v_k\big)-\tilde I\big(z_{(2)};v_k\big)\Big]. \tag{37} \] A factor captured by one coordinate scores close to its normalised mutual information. A factor smeared over two coordinates, say with 0.35 and 0.30, scores 0.05 even if the representation contains it completely. A factor copied into two coordinates, with 0.60 and 0.60, scores zero, although a score based on the largest value alone would reward it. The gap therefore rewards one coordinate per factor, and only one, which is what disentanglement asks for.

We estimate these quantities from histograms. For every active coordinate we put the posterior means \(\mu_k\) of the 5000 test trajectories into 20 bins of equal width between their smallest and largest value. Every factor (\(\omega\), \(\ln\gamma\), \(x_0\) and \(v_0\)) goes into the 20 equal-population bins described earlier. The probabilities in equations (34) and (35) are the frequencies of the bins and of the \(20\times20\) pairs of bins. Only active coordinates enter, because an inactive coordinate passes no information to the decoder, and a factor with fewer than two active coordinates is compared with zero for the missing ones. Chen et al. estimate the mutual information by sampling from \(q_\phi(\mathbf z|\mathbf x)\), and the binned means are a common simplification. With 20 bins the estimator is biased, and even a perfect code stays below 1 because the bins of the latent means and of the factors do not coincide. We therefore also evaluate the MIG for the true factors used as a code, and for the factors with \(\omega\) and \(x_0\) mixed by a rotation of \(45^\circ\). These two values set the scale between a perfect and a half-mixed code. We also compare one mutual information with mutual_info_score of scikit-learn.

    """|Pearson correlation| between every latent mean (columns of mu) and every factor (columns of P): (d, K)."""
    d = mu.shape[1]
    return np.nan_to_num(np.abs(np.corrcoef(np.c_[mu, P].T)[:d, d:]))

N_BINS = 20
def bins_equal_width(v):
    return np.clip(((v - v.min()) / (v.max() - v.min() + 1e-12) * N_BINS).astype(int), 0, N_BINS - 1)
def bins_quantile(v):
    return np.searchsorted(np.quantile(v, np.linspace(0, 1, N_BINS + 1)[1:-1]), v)
def entropy(labels):
    p = np.bincount(labels) / len(labels); p = p[p > 0]
    return float(-(p * np.log(p)).sum())
def mutual_information(a, b):
    return entropy(a) + entropy(b) - entropy(a * N_BINS + b)

def mig(mu, P, act):
    """Mutual information gap per factor; latent means of the active coordinates in 20 equal-width bins,
    factors in 20 equal-population bins (entropy ln 20)."""
    zb = [bins_equal_width(mu[:, j]) for j in act]
    gaps = []
    for k in range(P.shape[1]):
        vb = bins_quantile(P[:, k])
        I = sorted([mutual_information(z, vb) for z in zb] + [0.0, 0.0], reverse=True)
        gaps.append((I[0] - I[1]) / entropy(vb))
    return np.array(gaps)

Frequency and damping coordinates

A correlation coefficient compresses a cloud of points into one number, and scatter plots show the clouds themselves. We plot the coordinate most correlated with \(\omega\) against \(\omega\), coloured by \(x_0\), and the coordinate most correlated with \(\ln\gamma\) against \(\ln\gamma\), coloured by the regime. We measure how much of the variance of the frequency coordinate \(y\) a least-squares fit \(\hat y\) with a given set of factors explains by the coefficient of determination \[ R^2=1-\frac{\mathrm{Var}(y-\hat y)}{\mathrm{Var}\,y}, \tag{39} \] which is 1 for a perfect fit and 0 for a fit no better than a constant. We compute it with a least-squares solver and check it with LinearRegression of scikit-learn. We also compute the correlations separately in the underdamped and in the overdamped regime, where equation (10) predicts that only \(\omega^2/\gamma\) matters.

Physically the accuracy with which the encoder can measure the frequency is limited by how visible the oscillation is, and the amplitude of the oscillation is set by \(x_0\).

Sensitivity of a trajectory to the parameters

A network can separate only what the data separate. This depends on how strongly a trajectory responds to each factor and on whether two factors change it in different ways. For the Gaussian likelihood (25) with the mean trajectory \(\langle\mathbf x\rangle(\mathbf p)\) and the parameter vector \(\mathbf p\) made of \(\omega\), \(\ln\gamma\), \(x_0\) and \(v_0\), both properties follow from the Fisher information matrix \[ F_{ab}=\frac{1}{\sigma_x^2}\sum_{i=1}^T\frac{\partial\langle x(t_i)\rangle}{\partial p_a}\,\frac{\partial\langle x(t_i)\rangle}{\partial p_b}. \tag{40} \] It measures how much information about the parameters one noisy trajectory carries, and its inverse bounds the variance of any unbiased estimate of them (the Cramér–Rao bound). Its diagonal tells by how many \(\sigma_x\) the trajectory moves when one parameter changes. We define the visibility \[ V_a=\sqrt{F_{aa}}\;\mathrm{std}(p_a), \tag{41} \] the change of the whole trajectory, in units of \(\sigma_x\), for a change of \(p_a\) by one standard deviation of its distribution in the data. The normalised off-diagonal element \[ c_{ab}=\frac{F_{ab}}{\sqrt{F_{aa}F_{bb}}} \tag{42} \] is the cosine of the angle between the two sensitivity vectors, the derivatives of the mean trajectory with respect to \(p_a\) and \(p_b\). If it is close to \(\pm1\), a small change of \(p_a\) can be compensated by a change of \(p_b\). The data then hardly distinguish the two directions, and a network has no reason to put them into different coordinates. We obtain the derivatives of the mean trajectory (11) by forward-mode automatic differentiation (torch.func.jacfwd) at 2000 test parameter points and report medians over these points.

The same analysis explains the choice of the parameter ranges. With many oscillation periods in the window, the derivative of the trajectory with respect to \(\omega\) grows in time and oscillates. Trajectories for neighbouring frequencies then separate quickly, the map from \(\omega\) to the trajectory folds on itself, and a Gaussian code with a smooth decoder cannot follow it. With initial positions of both signs, the trajectories for \((\omega,\gamma,x_0,v_0)\) and for \((\omega,\gamma,-x_0,-v_0)\) differ only by a sign, and the code has to fold again.

The β-VAE thus finds the number of independent factors and, in all models, the damping. It finds the frequency and the initial conditions only as mixtures. The parameter that is most visible and least confusable with the others is the one that ends up in its own coordinate.

Question-and-answer networks

SciNet of Iten et al.

Iten, Metger, Wilming, del Rio and Renner, Phys. Rev. Lett. 124, 010508 (2020) proposed a network modelled on how a physicist works. Observations are compressed into a few parameters, and these parameters are used to answer questions about the system. Their network, SciNet, has three parts (their Fig. 1). A feed-forward encoder maps an observation \(o\) to a small latent representation. A question \(q\), encoded as real numbers, is appended to the latent values, and a feed-forward decoder maps the representation together with the question to an answer \(a\). Training uses triples of an observation, a question and the correct answer. Unlike in an autoencoder, the latent representation has to contain only what is needed to answer the questions. To make the latent neurons statistically independent, the authors use the β-VAE cost. The encoder outputs means and standard deviations of independent Gaussians, and with a Gaussian decoder of fixed variance the cost becomes (their Eq. (D1), Supplemental Material) \[ C_\beta=\lVert\hat a-a\rVert_2^2-\frac\beta2\sum_i\big(\log\sigma_i^2-\mu_i^2-\sigma_i^2\big)+\text{const}=\lVert\hat a-a\rVert_2^2+\beta\,\mathrm{KL}+\text{const}. \tag{43} \] The KL term is that of equation (28), and the reconstruction is measured on the answer.

Their first example (Box 1 and Sec. E1 of the Supplemental Material, arXiv:1807.10300) is a damped pendulum without noise, \[ m\ddot x=-\kappa x-b\dot x, \tag{44} \] with the mass \(m=1\,\)kg, the amplitude \(A_0=1\,\)m and the phase \(\delta_0=0\) fixed, so that \[ x(t)=A_0\,e^{-bt/(2m)}\cos(\omega t+\delta_0),\qquad\omega=\sqrt{\frac\kappa m}\,\sqrt{1-\frac{b^2}{4m\kappa}}. \tag{45} \] The spring constant \(\kappa\) varies between samples from 5 to 10 kg/s², the damping factor \(b\) from 0.5 to 1 kg/s. The observation is the position at 50 equally spaced times between 0 and 5 s, the question is a prediction time between 0 and 10 s, and the answer is the position at that time. The encoder has hidden layers of 500 and 100 neurons and the decoder two layers of 100, all with the exponential linear unit \[ \mathrm{ELU}(u)=\begin{cases}u,&u>0,\\e^u-1,&u\le0,\end{cases} \tag{46} \] a smooth variant of ReLU, and there are three latent neurons (their Table I). Training used 95 000 samples, batch size 512, learning rate \(10^{-3}\), \(\beta=10^{-3}\) and 1000 epochs (their Table II). The authors report predictions with a root-mean-square error below 2% of the amplitude. They find that the network stores \(\kappa\) and \(b\) in two latent neurons, the first depending nearly linearly on \(b\) and almost not on \(\kappa\), the second only on \(\kappa\), while the third neuron carries no information (their Fig. 6).

The setting differs from that of our β-VAE in three ways. The decoder answers one question at a time instead of reproducing the whole input, and the questions reach beyond the observed window, so the code must allow extrapolation. The data contain no noise and no nuisance factors, because with fixed amplitude and phase only \(\kappa\) and \(b\) vary. Finally, \(\beta=10^{-3}\) is small in their units. The KL term breaks the rotational symmetry of the latent space but hardly limits the information.

Network and cost

Our implementation follows Tables I and II of the paper for the layer sizes, the activation, the batch size, the learning rate and \(\beta\). We pair each observation with 8 random question times per training step, so one minibatch contains 4096 triples that share 512 samples of \(\mathbf z\). The cost is the mean of the squared answer error over the triples plus \(\beta\) times the KL. We train for 100 epochs on 20 000 observations instead of 1000 epochs on 95 000, about 4000 gradient steps instead of about 186 000, and this difference matters when comparing results. In the setting of the paper we therefore also train SciNet for 1000 epochs, about 39 000 steps. With random initial conditions the representation needs two more numbers, so that version gets five latent neurons instead of three. Iten et al. show that the autoencoder is the special case of SciNet with a single question, whose answer is the observation itself. The two values of β are on different scales. SciNet weighs the KL against the squared error of one answer with β of \(10^{-3}\). Our β-VAE divides the squared error of the whole observation by \(2\sigma_x^2\), which is 0.02, before adding β times the KL. Multiplying its loss by 0.02 gives the squared error of the observation plus 0.02β times the KL, which for β of 4 is 0.08 times the KL against the squared errors of all points, or 0.0016 per point for the 50 observed points. Per predicted position the weights of the KL are therefore of the same order in both networks, and the numbers 4 and \(10^{-3}\) should not be compared directly.

class SciNet(nn.Module):
    """Question-and-answer network in the style of Iten et al., with ELU activations.
    encoder: observation o (B, 50) -> 500 -> 100 -> mu (B, d), logvar (B, d)
    decoder: [z, t] (B, Q, d + 1)  -> 100 -> 100 -> answer x(t) (B, Q)"""
    def __init__(self, n_obs=N_OBS, d=3):
        super().__init__()
        self.enc = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(n_obs, 500), nn.ELU(), nn.Linear(500, 100), nn.ELU())
        self.mu, self.logvar = nn.Linear(100, d), nn.Linear(100, d)
        self.dec = nn.Sequential(nn.Linear(d + 1, 100), nn.ELU(), nn.Linear(100, 100), nn.ELU(), nn.Linear(100, 1))
    def encode(self, o):
        h = self.enc(o)
        return self.mu(h), self.logvar(h)
    def answer(self, z, q):
        """z (B, d), question times q (B, Q) -> predicted positions (B, Q)."""
        zq = torch.cat([z[:, None, :].expand(-1, q.shape[1], -1), q[..., None]], -1)
        return self.dec(zq)[..., 0]
    def forward(self, o, q):
        mu, logvar = self.encode(o)
        return self.answer(reparametrise(mu, logvar), q), mu, logvar

T_Q = torch.tensor(t_q, dtype=torch.float32)
def scinet_loss(model, batch, beta=1e-3, n_q=8):
    """Cost of Iten et al. per (observation, question, answer) triple: (x_hat - x)^2 + beta KL;
    n_q random questions per observation share one sample z."""
    o, a = batch                                   # observation (B, 50), answers on all question times (B, 101)
    mu, logvar = model.encode(o)
    idx = torch.randint(0, a.shape[1], (o.shape[0], n_q))
    pred = model.answer(reparametrise(mu, logvar), T_Q[idx])
    rec = ((pred - torch.gather(a, 1, idx)) ** 2).mean()
    kl = kl_per_dim(mu, logvar).sum(1).mean()
    return rec + beta * kl, {"loss": rec + beta * kl, "rec": rec, "kl": kl}

Two coupled oscillators

Normal modes

Two equal masses \(m\) are each attached to a wall by a spring \(k\) and to each other by a coupling spring \(k_c\). Both move in the same fluid with friction rate \(\gamma\), and each is hit by its own random force, \[ \begin{aligned} m\ddot x_1&=-kx_1-k_c(x_1-x_2)-m\gamma\dot x_1+m\sqrt{2D}\,\xi_1(t),\\ m\ddot x_2&=-kx_2-k_c(x_2-x_1)-m\gamma\dot x_2+m\sqrt{2D}\,\xi_2(t), \end{aligned} \tag{49} \] with independent white noises \(\xi_1\) and \(\xi_2\). In matrix form the restoring forces are given by the stiffness matrix \[ K=\begin{pmatrix}k+k_c&-k_c\\-k_c&k+k_c\end{pmatrix}. \tag{50} \] Its eigenvectors are the normal modes. In the in-phase motion along \((1,1)\) the coupling spring is never stretched, and in the anti-phase motion along \((1,-1)\) it is stretched twice as much as each mass moves. The two mode frequencies are \[ \omega_1=\sqrt{k/m},\qquad\omega_2=\sqrt{(k+2k_c)/m}. \tag{51} \] We find them with SymPy and confirm them with numpy.linalg.eigvalsh for random spring constants.

In the mode coordinates \[ q_\pm=\frac{x_1\pm x_2}{\sqrt2} \tag{52} \] the two equations separate, \[ \ddot q_+=-\omega_1^2q_+-\gamma\dot q_++\sqrt{2D}\,\xi_+,\qquad\ddot q_-=-\omega_2^2q_--\gamma\dot q_-+\sqrt{2D}\,\xi_-,\qquad\xi_\pm=\frac{\xi_1\pm\xi_2}{\sqrt2}. \tag{53} \] The friction is the same for both masses, so it does not couple the modes, and \(\xi_\pm\) are again independent white noises of unit strength, because \[ \langle\xi_+\xi_-\rangle=\tfrac12\big(\langle\xi_1^2\rangle-\langle\xi_2^2\rangle\big)=0. \tag{54} \] The two masses are therefore two independent noisy oscillators of the kind of equation (1), seen through a rotation of \(45^\circ\), and a trajectory depends on the springs only through \(\omega_1\) and \(\omega_2\). We run the Euler–Maruyama integrator of the two masses without noise from \(x_1=1\) and \(x_2=0.2\) at rest, and compare \(q_\pm\) with the exact damped oscillators at \(\omega_1\) and \(\omega_2\) (unit mass).

def simulate_two(k, kc, gamma, x10, x20, v10, v20, rng, D=D_NOISE, dt=DT, n_sub=N_SUB, n_rec=T_POINTS):
    """Euler-Maruyama for N pairs of coupled masses (m = 1); all parameters (N,). Returns positions (N, 2, n_rec)."""
    x1, x2, v1, v2 = (np.array(a, float) for a in (x10, x20, v10, v20))
    X = np.empty((len(x1), 2, n_rec)); X[:, 0, 0], X[:, 1, 0] = x1, x2
    kick = np.sqrt(2 * D * dt)
    for i in range(1, n_rec):
        for _ in range(n_sub):
            a1 = -k * x1 - kc * (x1 - x2) - gamma * v1
            a2 = -k * x2 - kc * (x2 - x1) - gamma * v2
            e1, e2 = rng.standard_normal((2, len(x1)))               # independent noises on the two masses
            x1, x2, v1, v2 = x1 + v1 * dt, x2 + v2 * dt, v1 + a1 * dt + kick * e1, v2 + a2 * dt + kick * e2
        X[:, 0, i], X[:, 1, i] = x1, x2
    return X

Summary

We integrated the Langevin equation (1) of a damped oscillator with white noise by the Euler–Maruyama scheme, whose noise term scales as the square root of the time step. Its mean and stationary variance agree with the exact results (11) and (19) up to an error of order \(\Delta t\), about 5% for the variance at \(\Delta t=0.01\).

A β-VAE with a Gaussian decoder, trained on 20 000 noisy trajectories without labels, uses four latent coordinates for the four factors for \(\beta\) between 0.5 and 4 in every seed, and at \(\beta=8\) it drops the initial velocity, the least visible factor. The damping is the one physical parameter that ends up in a coordinate of its own, with \(|\rho|\) between 0.80 and 0.83 averaged over seeds. In the overdamped regime this coordinate follows \(\omega^2/\gamma\), the slow decay rate, which is the combination the data contain. The frequency is stored together with the initial position, because the accuracy with which the encoder can measure a frequency is limited by how visible the oscillation is. The mutual information gap stays between 0.040 and 0.130 for all \(\beta\) and seeds, against 0.916 for the true factors. The Fisher information of the trajectories explains this ranking. The visibility of a parameter decides whether it is stored, and the overlap of the sensitivity directions together with the nonlinearity of the map decides whether it is stored in a coordinate of its own.

The question-and-answer network SciNet of Iten et al. reproduces their result, one neuron for \(\kappa\) and one for \(b\) with \(|\rho|\) of 0.99 and 0.98 and a prediction error of 1.8% after 1000 epochs, and the β-VAE finds the same two parameters with a stronger KL term after 60 epochs. Weak noise changes little, while nuisance initial conditions change which parameters are stored first. For two coupled masses the β-VAE stores the anti-phase normal-mode frequency \(\omega_2\) in one coordinate (\(|\rho|\) of 0.80, against 0.52 with the coupling spring) and has no coordinate of its own for the in-phase frequency.

A latent coordinate thus becomes interpretable when its parameter is visible in the data and no other parameter changes the data in a similar way, and a coordinate has to be checked against known physics before it is given a name.

Exercise 1: Euler–Maruyama for a free Brownian particle

A free overdamped Brownian particle obeys \[ \dot x=\sqrt{2D}\,\xi(t). \tag{57} \] (a) Write the Euler–Maruyama update and show that, for a start at the origin, the mean squared displacement after \(n\) steps is given by equation (59) for every step size \(\Delta t\). (b) Explain why the noise term must scale as \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\), by computing the mean squared displacement at a fixed time for a noise term that scales as \(\Delta t\). (c) Check (a) numerically for two step sizes.

Solution

  1. The update and its solution are \[ x_{n+1}=x_n+\sqrt{2D\,\Delta t}\,\eta_n,\qquad x_n=\sqrt{2D\,\Delta t}\sum_{j<n}\eta_j, \tag{58} \] and since the \(\eta_j\) are independent with unit variance, \[ \langle x_n^2\rangle=2D\,\Delta t\,n=2Dt_n. \tag{59} \] The scheme is exact for this equation at any \(\Delta t\). (b) With a noise term proportional to \(\Delta t\,\eta_n\) we would get \[ \langle x_n^2\rangle\propto n\,\Delta t^2=t_n\,\Delta t, \tag{60} \] which vanishes as the step size goes to zero, so the particle would stop diffusing in the continuum limit. Only the factor \(\sqrt{\Delta t}\) gives a limit independent of the step size. (c) The simulation uses \(D=0.5\) and the time 2, where equation (59) gives 2.

Replace the spring by a double-well potential, with the equation of motion \[ V(x)=a\,(x^2-1)^2,\qquad\ddot x=-V'(x)-\gamma\dot x+\sqrt{2D}\,\xi(t), \tag{68} \] and vary the barrier height \(a\) and the temperature, given by the Einstein relation (4) with unit mass, between trajectories. Choose a time window long enough to see several jumps between the wells. The mean time between jumps follows Kramers’ law [Kramers 1940, Hänggi, Talkner and Borkovec 1990], \[ \tau\propto e^{a/k_BT}. \tag{69} \] Train a β-VAE on the trajectories and test whether a latent coordinate follows \(\ln\tau\), which is the ratio of \(a\) and \(k_BT\), or \(a\) and \(T\) separately. The time of the first jump is random and differs between trajectories with the same parameters, so the encoder must summarise statistics of the trajectory. A summary input such as the histogram of \(x\) or the sequence of well occupations may work better than the raw positions.
