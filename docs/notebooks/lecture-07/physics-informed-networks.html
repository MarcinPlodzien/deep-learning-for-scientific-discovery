Physics-informed neural networks

Deep Learning for Scientific Discovery, Jagiellonian University. Author Marcin Płodzień.

Differential equations whose form is known but whose coefficients are partly unknown appear in every natural science, for example the diffusion of heat or of a dissolved substance in tissue, soil or groundwater, rate equations in chemical kinetics and growth models in population dynamics. Measurements in such systems are often sparse and noisy, and a method that combines the law with a few measurements to estimate the solution and the unknown coefficients is useful across these fields.

A physics-informed neural network (PINN) represents the solution of a differential equation by a neural network and trains the network to satisfy the equation. The network \(x_\theta(t)\) is the solution itself. It receives the time \(t\) as input and returns the position of the system at that time. Its time derivatives follow from automatic differentiation, the application of the chain rule to the operations of the network, and they are the derivatives of the network function itself, with no discretisation of time. The loss adds a data part, which measures the distance of \(x_\theta\) from the initial conditions and, when available, from a few measurements, and the residual of the known law, which measures how far \(x_\theta\) is from satisfying the equation of motion at a set of sample times called collocation points. A neural ODE learns an unknown law from trajectories, while a PINN assumes the law and learns its solution. The idea goes back to Lagaris, Likas and Fotiadis [IEEE Trans. Neural Netw. 9, 987 (1998), doi:10.1109/72.712178], and Raissi, Perdikaris and Karniadakis gave it its present form and name [J. Comput. Phys. 378, 686 (2019), doi:10.1016/j.jcp.2018.10.045].

The construction is attractive when a physical law is known but its solution is wanted together with information that a classical solver cannot use directly. The network is a function of continuous coordinates, so the equation is enforced at arbitrary sample points without a mesh. Measurements enter the same loss as the equation, so a few sparse or noisy data points and the law together determine the solution, and an unknown coefficient of the law can be trained together with the network. Such inverse problems are the main application of PINNs. For a plain forward problem with known coefficients and known initial and boundary conditions a classical solver is faster and more accurate, and our experiments measure the size of this difference.

We work with three classical systems. The damped harmonic oscillator, a mass on a spring slowed down by friction, has an analytical solution, a formula for \(x(t)\), so every error of the network can be measured against it. The heat equation in a rod is a partial differential equation in space and time with a solution by separation of variables, a sum of products of a function of position and a function of time. The nonlinear pendulum has no solution in elementary functions, and we compare the network with a fourth-order Runge–Kutta (RK4) integration, a standard numerical method that advances the state from four evaluations of the right-hand side per step. We first describe the physics of the oscillator and the pendulum, their phase portraits, the curves traced by the state in the plane of position and velocity, and their energies. We then build the PINN, draw it, train it on the oscillator and compare its accuracy and its cost with two classical solvers, our own RK4 and the adaptive solver of the library diffrax. Two further experiments show what the physics term of the loss adds to a fit of sparse data and how the same construction estimates an unknown damping coefficient from noisy observations. The heat equation repeats the comparison for a partial differential equation, first against a finite-difference solver and then in the inverse problem of measuring the thermal diffusivity of the rod with a few thermometers. The pendulum over several periods exhibits failures of PINN training. The comparisons of cost with classical solvers, the check of our own Adam optimiser against the library and the training of the pendulum one period at a time, called time marching, go deeper than the lecture, which uses only their main results.

The implementation uses JAX in double precision. The network, its derivatives and the loss are written with jax.grad, jax.hessian and jax.vmap, the optimisers come from optax and are checked against our own Adam, and the classical integrations use our RK4 next to diffrax.

1. Damped harmonic oscillator

A mass \(m\) on a spring of stiffness \(k\) that moves through a viscous medium, such as oil or air, with friction coefficient \(c\) obeys Newton’s equation \[m\ddot x+c\dot x+kx=0,\qquad x(0)=x_0,\quad \dot x(0)=v_0 . \tag{1}\] The friction term \(c\dot x\) is the damping. It opposes the velocity and turns the energy of the motion into heat, so the oscillation decays. With the decay rate \(\gamma=c/2m\), the natural angular frequency \(\omega_0=\sqrt{k/m}\) and the damping ratio \(\zeta=\gamma/\omega_0\), the motion is oscillatory for \(\zeta<1\) (underdamped). The trial solution \(e^{\lambda t}\) gives \(\lambda=-\gamma\pm i\omega_d\) with \(\omega_d=\omega_0\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}\), and the solution with the given initial conditions is \[x(t)=e^{-\gamma t}\Big[x_0\cos\omega_dt+\frac{v_0+\gamma x_0}{\omega_d}\sin\omega_dt\Big]. \tag{2}\] The mechanical energy \(E=\tfrac12m\dot x^2+\tfrac12kx^2\) is not conserved. Multiplying the equation of motion by \(\dot x\) gives \[\frac{dE}{dt}=-c\,\dot x^2\le0 , \tag{3}\] so friction removes energy at every instant except the turning points, where \(\dot x=0\). Averaged over one period the energy decays as \(e^{-2\gamma t}\), twice the rate of the amplitude.

We take \(m=1\), \(c=0.4\), \(k=4\), \(x_0=1\), \(v_0=0\) and the time window \(0\le t\le10\). We evaluate \(\zeta\), \(\omega_d\) and the period \(2\pi/\omega_d\), check the analytical velocity \(\dot x(t)\) against a finite difference of \(x(t)\), and compare the energy at the end of the window with \(e^{-2\gamma t}\).

M, C_TRUE, K = 1.0, 0.4, 4.0          # mass, damping coefficient, spring constant
X0, V0, T_END = 1.0, 0.0, 10.0        # initial position and velocity, end of the time window
GAMMA = C_TRUE / (2 * M)              # decay rate of the amplitude
OMEGA0 = np.sqrt(K / M)               # natural angular frequency
ZETA = GAMMA / OMEGA0                 # damping ratio, < 1 for the underdamped oscillator
OMEGA_D = OMEGA0 * np.sqrt(1 - ZETA**2)

def x_exact(t, c=C_TRUE, x0=X0, v0=V0):
    # underdamped solution with x(0) = x0, xdot(0) = v0
    g = c / (2 * M); wd = np.sqrt(K / M - g**2)
    return np.exp(-g * t) * (x0 * np.cos(wd * t) + (v0 + g * x0) / wd * np.sin(wd * t))

def v_exact(t, c=C_TRUE, x0=X0, v0=V0):
    g = c / (2 * M); wd = np.sqrt(K / M - g**2)
    return np.exp(-g * t) * (v0 * np.cos(wd * t) - (g * v0 + (g**2 + wd**2) * x0) / wd * np.sin(wd * t))

def energy(x, v):
    return 0.5 * M * v**2 + 0.5 * K * x**2

Nonlinear pendulum

A point mass on a massless rod of length \(\ell\) in the gravitational field \(g\) moves according to \[\ddot\theta=-\omega_p^2\sin\theta,\qquad \omega_p^2=g/\ell , \tag{4}\] where \(\theta\) is the angle from the downward vertical. For small angles \(\sin\theta\approx\theta\) and the pendulum is a harmonic oscillator of angular frequency \(\omega_p\). For finite angles the restoring force grows more slowly than \(\theta\) and the period becomes longer. Without friction the energy per unit \(m\ell^2\), \[E=\tfrac12\dot\theta^2-\omega_p^2\cos\theta , \tag{5}\] is conserved, so every trajectory lies on a contour line of \(E\) in the phase plane \((\theta,\dot\theta)\). The contour \(E=\omega_p^2\) is the separatrix. It passes through the unstable upright position \(\theta=\pm\pi\) and separates the oscillations (\(E<\omega_p^2\)) from the rotations over the top (\(E>\omega_p^2\)).

The equation has no solution in elementary functions. For a pendulum released at rest from \(\theta_0\) the energy conservation gives the period \(T_p\) as an integral, which becomes [S. H. Strogatz, Nonlinear Dynamics and Chaos, 2nd ed., CRC Press (2018), doi:10.1201/9780429492563] \[T_p=\frac{4}{\omega_p}K(q),\qquad q=\sin\frac{\theta_0}{2},\qquad K(q)=\int_0^{\pi/2}\frac{d\eta}{\sqrt{1-q^2\sin^2\eta}} , \tag{6}\] with \(K\) the complete elliptic integral of the first kind and \(q\) its modulus. We measure time in units of \(1/\omega_p\), set \(\omega_p=1\), and release the pendulum from \(\theta_0=2\) rad (about 115 degrees) at rest. The reference solution comes from our RK4 integrator with step \(h=10^{-3}\). One RK4 step for \(\dot{\mathbf y}=\mathbf f(\mathbf y)\) evaluates the right-hand side four times, \[\mathbf k_1=\mathbf f(\mathbf y_n),\ \ \mathbf k_2=\mathbf f\big(\mathbf y_n+\tfrac h2\mathbf k_1\big),\ \ \mathbf k_3=\mathbf f\big(\mathbf y_n+\tfrac h2\mathbf k_2\big),\ \ \mathbf k_4=\mathbf f(\mathbf y_n+h\mathbf k_3), \tag{7}\] \[\mathbf y_{n+1}=\mathbf y_n+\tfrac h6(\mathbf k_1+2\mathbf k_2+2\mathbf k_3+\mathbf k_4), \tag{8}\] and its global error decreases as \(h^4\) [E. Hairer, S. P. Nørsett, G. Wanner, Solving Ordinary Differential Equations I, 2nd ed., Springer (1993), doi:10.1007/978-3-540-78862-1]. We check the period from the elliptic integral against the times between successive maxima of the RK4 solution, and we monitor the conservation of \(E\).

OMEGA_P2, TH0 = 1.0, 2.0              # g/l (time unit 1/omega_p) and initial angle in rad, started at rest
Q_ELL = np.sin(TH0 / 2)               # elliptic modulus q = sin(theta_0/2)
T_PEND = 4 * ellipk(Q_ELL**2) / np.sqrt(OMEGA_P2)     # period, scipy's ellipk takes the parameter q^2

def rk4_step(f, y, h):
    # one classical fourth-order Runge-Kutta step for dy/dt = f(y)
    k1 = f(y); k2 = f(y + 0.5 * h * k1); k3 = f(y + 0.5 * h * k2); k4 = f(y + h * k3)
    return y + h / 6 * (k1 + 2 * k2 + 2 * k3 + k4)

def rk4_solve(f, y0, h, n):
    # n RK4 steps of size h, returns the states at t = 0, h, ..., n h
    _, ys = jax.lax.scan(lambda y, _: (rk4_step(f, y, h),) * 2, jnp.asarray(y0), None, length=n)
    return jnp.concatenate([jnp.asarray(y0)[None], ys])

2. Physics-informed neural network

A neural ODE learns a map from a state to a velocity and uses a numerical solver to construct trajectories. Here the equation is given and the unknown is one solution curve. The PINN maps a time directly to a position. Every choice of network weights proposes a whole curve, and training checks that curve against the equation and the information that selects the required motion. This basic PINN represents one initial-value problem on a chosen interval; changing the initial conditions generally requires another fit.

The inverse experiments also reconstruct the trajectory or field. That reconstruction from a model and incomplete observations is data assimilation.

The PINN replaces the unknown function \(x(t)\) by a multilayer perceptron \(x_\theta(t)\) with parameters \(\theta\). The input \(t\in[0,T]\) is first rescaled to \(\tau=2t/T-1\in[-1,1]\), then passes through three hidden layers of 32 tanh units each, and a linear output unit returns the number \(x_\theta(t)\), \[\mathbf h_1=\tanh(W_1\tau+\mathbf b_1),\qquad \mathbf h_{l+1}=\tanh(W_{l+1}\mathbf h_l+\mathbf b_{l+1}),\qquad x_\theta(t)=W_4\mathbf h_3+b_4 . \tag{9}\] Because the network is a composition of smooth functions, its derivatives \(\dot x_\theta=\partial_tx_\theta\) and \(\ddot x_\theta=\partial_t^2x_\theta\) exist at every \(t\). Automatic differentiation evaluates them by applying the chain rule to the same composition, so they equal the derivatives of the network function up to round-off and involve no finite differences. With them we form the residual of the equation of motion, \[r_\theta(t)=\frac{m\ddot x_\theta(t)+c\dot x_\theta(t)+kx_\theta(t)}{k}, \tag{10}\] which vanishes for every \(t\) when \(x_\theta\) solves the equation. Dividing by \(k\) gives the residual the units of a position.

def init_mlp(key, sizes):
    # LeCun normal weights (variance 1/n_in) and zero biases, one (W, b) pair per layer
    params = []
    for n_in, n_out in zip(sizes[:-1], sizes[1:]):
        key, sub = jax.random.split(key)
        params.append((jax.random.normal(sub, (n_out, n_in)) / np.sqrt(n_in), jnp.zeros(n_out)))
    return params

def make_net(t_a, t_b):
    # scalar network x_theta(t) on the window [t_a, t_b]; the input is rescaled to [-1, 1]
    def net(params, t):
        h = jnp.atleast_1d(2 * (t - t_a) / (t_b - t_a) - 1)
        for W, b in params[:-1]:
            h = jnp.tanh(W @ h + b)
        W, b = params[-1]
        return (W @ h + b)[0]
    d1 = jax.grad(net, argnums=1)          # dx_theta/dt
    d2 = jax.grad(d1, argnums=1)           # d^2x_theta/dt^2
    return net, d1, d2

def n_params(params):
    return int(sum(W.size + b.size for W, b in params))

Loss

This forward-solving experiment supplies no measured solution values. Later experiments add observations. It consists of \(N_r=200\) collocation points \(t_i\), times chosen to evaluate how well the candidate curve satisfies the equation, equally spaced on \([0,10]\), and of the two initial conditions. The loss is \[\mathcal L(\theta)=\frac{w_r}{N_r}\sum_{i=1}^{N_r}r_\theta(t_i)^2+w_0\big[x_\theta(0)-x_0\big]^2+w_1\Big[\frac{\dot x_\theta(0)-v_0}{\omega_0}\Big]^2 . \tag{11}\] The first term is the mean squared residual. Each collocation point tests the equation of motion with the target residual zero. Adding points samples this test more densely. The division by \(N_r\) keeps their number from automatically increasing the residual weight. The residual term is zero for every solution of the equation of motion, a two-parameter family of damped oscillations, and therefore cannot select one of them. The two initial-condition terms select the solution with \(x(0)=x_0\) and \(\dot x(0)=v_0\), and the division by \(\omega_0\) gives the velocity term the units of a position. The weights \(w_r\), \(w_0\) and \(w_1\) fix the balance between the terms. A small weight of the initial conditions lets the optimiser settle on a function with a small residual that starts at the wrong state, and a large one slows down the reduction of the residual. We use \(w_r=w_0=w_1=1\), a choice that works for this problem. Wang, Teng and Perdikaris adapt the weights during training from the sizes of the gradients of the separate terms [arXiv:2001.04536]. When measurements \(x_j\) at times \(t_j\) are available, a data term \[\mathcal L_{\rm d}(\theta)=\frac{w_d}{N_d}\sum_j[x_\theta(t_j)-x_j]^2 \tag{12}\] is added in the same way.

Lagaris et al. satisfied the initial conditions by construction with the ansatz \[x_\theta(t)=x_0+v_0t+t^2N_\theta(t), \tag{13}\] where \(N_\theta\) is the network. This removes the initial-condition terms, and we keep the penalty form because it carries over to the inverse problem and to data terms without change.

One update evaluates the curve and its time derivatives, forms the required loss terms, differentiates the loss with respect to the weights, and changes those weights. All times contribute to the same optimisation, and no RK4 time stepping occurs inside this loss. Once trained, the network is evaluated directly at the requested time.

Zero residual throughout the interval plus correct initial conditions identifies the exact solution. Zero residual at finitely many collocation points does not establish that condition between them. Check the residual at fresh times, the condition errors and, here, the deviation from the known solution. Predictions outside the collocation interval are extrapolations.

    """Loss of the oscillator PINN. With learn_c the damping c is the last entry of the parameter tuple."""
    def loss(params, c=C_TRUE):
        r = jax.vmap(lambda t: (M * d2(params, t) + c * d1(params, t) + K * net(params, t)) / K)(t_col)
        L = w_r * jnp.mean(r**2)
        if use_ic:

        if w_d > 0:
            L += w_d * jnp.mean((jax.vmap(lambda t: net(params, t))(t_data) - x_data)**2)
        return L
    return loss

Optimisers

We minimise \(\mathcal L\) in two stages. The first is the Adam optimiser (adaptive moment estimation) [Kingma and Ba, arXiv:1412.6980]. With the gradient \(\mathbf g_n=\nabla_\theta\mathcal L\) at step \(n\) it keeps running averages of the gradient and of its componentwise square, \[\mathbf m_n=\beta_1\mathbf m_{n-1}+(1-\beta_1)\mathbf g_n,\qquad \mathbf v_n=\beta_2\mathbf v_{n-1}+(1-\beta_2)\mathbf g_n^2, \tag{14}\] corrects their bias from the zero initial values, \(\hat{\mathbf m}_n=\mathbf m_n/(1-\beta_1^n)\) and \(\hat{\mathbf v}_n=\mathbf v_n/(1-\beta_2^n)\), and updates \(\theta_{n}=\theta_{n-1}-\eta\,\hat{\mathbf m}_n/(\sqrt{\hat{\mathbf v}_n}+\epsilon)\) with \(\beta_1=0.9\), \(\beta_2=0.999\) and \(\epsilon=10^{-8}\). Every parameter thus moves by a step of order \(\eta\) whatever the scale of its gradient. Our implementation of these four lines in JAX and optax.adam run for 300 steps from the same initial parameters with \(\eta=3\times10^{-3}\).

Code 
def adam_init(params):
    zeros = jax.tree.map(jnp.zeros_like, params)
    return dict(m=zeros, v=zeros, n=0)

def adam_update(grads, state, lr, b1=0.9, b2=0.999, eps=1e-8):
    # Adam: running means of the gradient and of its square, bias correction, step lr m_hat/(sqrt(v_hat)+eps)
    n = state["n"] + 1
    m = jax.tree.map(lambda m, g: b1 * m + (1 - b1) * g, state["m"], grads)
    v = jax.tree.map(lambda v, g: b2 * v + (1 - b2) * g**2, state["v"], grads)
    step = jax.tree.map(lambda m, v: -lr * (m / (1 - b1**n)) / (jnp.sqrt(v / (1 - b2**n)) + eps), m, v)
    return step, dict(m=m, v=v, n=n)

def train(loss, params, n_adam=10000, n_lbfgs=3000, lr=3e-3, monitor=None, every=250):
    """Adam with a cosine learning-rate decay, followed by L-BFGS. Returns parameters and a history."""
    tx = optax.adam(optax.cosine_decay_schedule(lr, n_adam, alpha=1e-2))
    state = tx.init(params)
    @jax.jit
    def adam_step(p, s):
        l, g = jax.value_and_grad(loss)(p)
        u, s = tx.update(g, s, p)
        return optax.apply_updates(p, u), s, l
    lb = optax.lbfgs()
    vg_state = optax.value_and_grad_from_state(loss)
    @jax.jit
    def lbfgs_step(p, s):
        l, g = vg_state(p, state=s)
        u, s = lb.update(g, s, p, value=l, grad=g, value_fn=loss)
        return optax.apply_updates(p, u), s, l
    hist = dict(it=[], loss=[], mon=[])
    t0 = time.time()
    for it in range(n_adam + n_lbfgs):
        if it == n_adam:
            state = lb.init(params)
        params, state, l = (adam_step if it < n_adam else lbfgs_step)(params, state)
        if it % every == 0 or it == n_adam + n_lbfgs - 1:
            hist["it"].append(it); hist["loss"].append(float(l))
            if monitor is not None:
                hist["mon"].append(monitor(params))
    hist = {k: np.array(v) for k, v in hist.items()}
    hist["time"] = time.time() - t0
    hist["final_loss"] = float(loss(params))
    return params, hist

3. Accuracy and cost

A PINN solves the same initial-value problem that a classical integrator solves, so the two can be compared directly. The classical solvers integrate the first-order system for the state \(\mathbf y=(x,\dot x)\), \[\dot{\mathbf y}=\mathbf f(\mathbf y)=\Big(\dot x,\ -\frac{c\dot x+kx}{m}\Big). \tag{15}\] We use our RK4 with a constant step \(h\) and two solvers of diffrax [P. Kidger, On Neural Differential Equations, PhD thesis, University of Oxford (2021), arXiv:2202.02435]. The first is RK4 itself, defined through its Butcher tableau as a diffrax solver, which must reproduce our integrator to round-off. The second is Tsitouras’ 5(4) Runge–Kutta pair (Tsit5) [Ch. Tsitouras, Comput. Math. Appl. 62, 770 (2011), doi:10.1016/j.camwa.2011.06.002] with adaptive step size. Each step of Tsit5 estimates its local error from the difference of a fifth- and a fourth-order solution, and a proportional–integral–derivative (PID) controller of diffrax shrinks or enlarges the step so that this estimate stays below the relative and absolute tolerances.

4. Inverse problem

In an experiment the parameters of the equation are often unknown, and the measurements are noisy. The PINN treats an unknown parameter like a weight of the network. We assume that \(m\) and \(k\) are known and the damping coefficient \(c\) is unknown, and we measure the position at 25 equally spaced times \(t_j\in[0,10]\) with independent Gaussian noise of standard deviation \(\sigma=0.02\), \[y_j=x(t_j)+\sigma\varepsilon_j,\qquad \varepsilon_j\sim\mathcal N(0,1). \tag{16}\] The initial state is treated as unknown as well, so the loss contains no initial-condition terms, \[\mathcal L(\theta,c)=\frac1{N_{\rm obs}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{\rm obs}}\big[x_\theta(t_j)-y_j\big]^2+\frac1{N_r}\sum_{i=1}^{N_r}r_{\theta,c}(t_i)^2, \tag{17}\] where the residual \(r_{\theta,c}\) uses the trainable \(c\). Both terms are in units of a squared position, so equal weights penalise a violation of the equation of motion like a deviation from the data of the same size. We write \(c=e^{s}\) and train \(s\), which keeps \(c\) positive, and start from \(c=1.0\), two and a half times the true value \(0.4\).

The classical reference is a least-squares fit of the analytical solution \(x(t;c,x_0,v_0)\) to the same data with scipy.optimize.least_squares. Its standard error of \(c\) follows from the Jacobian \(J\) of the normalised residuals \((x(t_j)-y_j)/\sigma\) as the square root of the diagonal element of \((J^\top J)^{-1}\). We repeat both estimates for five independent noise realisations.

The observations must contain information about damping. A motion that remained at \(x=0\) would satisfy the equation for every \(c\) and could not identify it. Optimising the residual alone therefore cannot replace informative data for this inverse problem.

        return fit + jnp.mean(r**2)          # both terms in units of x squared, equal weights

5. Heat equation

Diffusion in a rod

A thin rod conducts heat along its axis, and its two ends are held at the same fixed temperature. We measure the position \(x\) along the rod in units of its length and the temperature \(u(x,t)\) relative to that of the ends. Fourier’s law states that the heat current is proportional to \(-\partial_xu\), and the conservation of energy then gives the heat equation \[\partial_tu=D\,\partial_x^2u,\qquad 0<x<1,\quad 0<t\le t_{\max}, \tag{18}\] with the boundary and initial conditions \[u(0,t)=u(1,t)=0,\qquad u(x,0)=u_0(x). \tag{19}\] The thermal diffusivity \(D\) sets the time scale of the conduction. The same equation describes every diffusion process, with \(u\) a concentration and \(D\) the diffusion coefficient. Integrating the equation over the rod gives \[\frac{d}{dt}\int_0^1u\,dx=D\big[\partial_xu(1,t)-\partial_xu(0,t)\big], \tag{20}\] so the heat content of the rod changes only through the heat currents at its ends.

The product ansatz \(u=X(x)\,T(t)\) separates the equation into \(X''/X=\dot T/(DT)=-\lambda\). The boundary conditions admit only \(X_n(x)=\sin n\pi x\) with \(\lambda_n=n^2\pi^2\), \(n=1,2,\dots\), and every such mode decays exponentially in time, \[\begin{aligned} u(x,t)&=\sum_{n=1}^\infty b_n\,e^{-t/\tau_n}\sin n\pi x,\\ \tau_n&=\frac{1}{Dn^2\pi^2},\qquad b_n=2\int_0^1u_0(x)\sin n\pi x\,dx . \end{aligned} \tag{21}\] A mode with \(n\) half-wavelengths along the rod decays \(n^2\) times faster than the lowest mode, so fine structure in the temperature profile disappears first and the profile approaches the shape \(\sin\pi x\) while its amplitude decays.

We take \(D=0.1\), \(t_{\max}=1\) and the initial profile \(u_0(x)=\sin\pi x+\tfrac12\sin3\pi x\), for which \(b_1=1\), \(b_3=\tfrac12\) and all other coefficients vanish. We evaluate \(\tau_1\) and \(\tau_3\) and check with central finite differences that the series solves the heat equation.

Crank–Nicolson solver

The classical reference is a finite-difference solution on the grid \(x_i=i\,\Delta x\), \(\Delta x=1/N_x\), and \(t^n=n\,\Delta t\), \(\Delta t=t_{\max}/N_t\). The numbers \(U_i^n\) approximate \(u(x_i,t^n)\), and the second derivative is replaced by the second difference \(\delta^2U_i=U_{i+1}-2U_i+U_{i-1}\). The Crank–Nicolson scheme averages this difference over the old and the new time level [J. Crank and P. Nicolson, Proc. Cambridge Philos. Soc. 43, 50 (1947), doi:10.1017/S0305004100023197], \[\frac{U_i^{n+1}-U_i^n}{\Delta t}=\frac{D}{2\Delta x^2}\big(\delta^2U_i^{n+1}+\delta^2U_i^n\big),\qquad U_0^n=U_{N_x}^n=0 . \tag{22}\] Every time step solves a tridiagonal linear system for the \(N_x-1\) interior values, which costs a number of operations proportional to \(N_x\). The error decreases as \(\Delta x^2+\Delta t^2\), and the scheme is stable for every \(\Delta t\). The explicit scheme with the forward difference in time needs no linear solve but is stable only for \(D\Delta t/\Delta x^2\le\tfrac12\). We solve on grids with \(N_x=N_t=10,20,\dots,160\), measure the error as the largest deviation \(\max_{i,n}|U_i^n-u(x_i,t^n)|\) from the series solution over all grid nodes, and record the median wall time of the compiled solver.

Code 
def cn_solve(D, nx, nt):
    # Crank-Nicolson for u_t = D u_xx with u(0,t) = u(1,t) = 0; returns U[n, i] at t^n = n dt, x_i = i dx
    dx, dt = 1.0 / nx, T_MAX / nt
    a = 0.5 * D * dt / dx**2
    m = nx - 1                                             # number of interior nodes
    dl = jnp.full(m, -a).at[0].set(0.0); du = jnp.full(m, -a).at[-1].set(0.0); dd = jnp.full(m, 1 + 2 * a)
    def step(u, _):
        rhs = (1 - 2 * a) * u + a * (jnp.pad(u[1:], (0, 1)) + jnp.pad(u[:-1], (1, 0)))   # (1 + D dt/2 delta^2) U^n
        u = jax.lax.linalg.tridiagonal_solve(dl, dd, du, rhs[:, None])[:, 0]           # (1 - D dt/2 delta^2) U^{n+1} = rhs
        return u, u
    u_in = u0_heat(jnp.linspace(0.0, 1.0, nx + 1)[1:-1])
    _, us = jax.lax.scan(step, u_in, None, length=nt)
    return jnp.pad(jnp.concatenate([u_in[None], us]), ((0, 0), (1, 1)))     # add the boundary nodes

PINN of the heat equation

The network \(u_\theta(x,t)\) now has two inputs. Both are rescaled to \([-1,1]\), and the hidden layers are those of the oscillator, three layers of 32 tanh units. The residual of the heat equation is \[r_\theta(x,t)=\partial_tu_\theta(x,t)-D\,\partial_x^2u_\theta(x,t), \tag{23}\] where jax.grad with respect to \(t\) gives the time derivative and jax.hessian with respect to \(x\) the second space derivative. The loss contains three terms, \[\begin{aligned} \mathcal L(\theta)={}&\frac1{N_r}\sum_{i=1}^{N_r}r_\theta(x_i,t_i)^2+\frac1{N_0}\sum_{k=1}^{N_0}\big[u_\theta(x_k,0)-u_0(x_k)\big]^2\\ &+\frac1{N_b}\sum_{l=1}^{N_b}\big[u_\theta(0,t_l)^2+u_\theta(1,t_l)^2\big]. \end{aligned} \tag{24}\] The first term is the mean squared residual at \(N_r=41\times41=1681\) collocation points \((x_i,t_i)\) on a regular grid of the domain \([0,1]\times[0,t_{\max}]\). The second term enforces the initial profile at \(N_0=101\) equally spaced positions \(x_k\), and the third term the boundary conditions at \(N_b=51\) equally spaced times \(t_l\) at both ends. All weights equal one. Every loss evaluation costs about eight times as many network passes as for the oscillator, and we shorten the training to 5000 Adam steps with the cosine schedule and 2000 L-BFGS iterations. The error is the largest deviation \(\max|u_\theta-u|\) from the series solution on a grid of \(101\times101\) points.

def heat_net(params, x, t):
    # network u_theta(x, t); both inputs rescaled to [-1, 1]
    h = jnp.array([2 * x - 1, 2 * t / T_MAX - 1])

        h = jnp.tanh(W @ h + b)

    return loss

Thermal diffusivity from four thermometers

In the inverse problem the diffusivity \(D\) is unknown, and four thermometers at the fixed positions \(x_s=0.1,\,0.3,\,0.55,\,0.8\) record the temperature at the 20 times \(t_j=0.05\,j\), \(j=1,\dots,20\). The readings carry independent Gaussian noise of standard deviation \(\sigma=0.01\), \[y_{sj}=u(x_s,t_j)+\sigma\varepsilon_{sj},\qquad \varepsilon_{sj}\sim\mathcal N(0,1). \tag{25}\] The initial profile and the temperature of the ends are known. The loss of the forward problem receives the data term \[\mathcal L_{\rm data}(\theta)=\frac1{N_{\rm obs}}\sum_{s,j}\big[u_\theta(x_s,t_j)-y_{sj}\big]^2,\qquad N_{\rm obs}=80, \tag{26}\] and the residual uses the trainable diffusivity \(D=e^{s}\), written through its logarithm \(s\) so that it stays positive. Training starts from \(D=0.3\), three times the true value, with the same budget as the forward problem. The trained network is at the same time an estimate of the whole temperature field \(u(x,t)\) that is consistent with the heat equation and with the sparse readings, a simple example of data assimilation, the estimation of the state of a system from a model combined with incomplete measurements.

The classical reference fits \(D\) through the Crank–Nicolson solver. On the grid \(N_x=100\), \(N_t=200\) the thermometer positions and reading times are grid nodes, and scipy.optimize.least_squares minimises the sum of the squared normalised deviations \([U(x_s,t_j;D)-y_{sj}]/\sigma\) over \(D\). Its standard error follows from the Jacobian as for the oscillator. We repeat both estimates for five noise realisations.

6. Pendulum over several periods

For the pendulum the network \(\theta_{\rm PINN}(t)\) has the residual \(r(t)=\ddot\theta_{\rm PINN}(t)+\omega_p^2\sin\theta_{\rm PINN}(t)\), and the loss contains its mean square and the initial conditions \(\theta_{\rm PINN}(0)=\theta_0\), \(\dot\theta_{\rm PINN}(0)=0\) with unit weights. We keep the network, the optimiser and the training budget of the oscillator, place 100 collocation points per period and train on windows of one, two and four periods \(T_p\), each with three random initialisations (seeds). A second experiment trains in time order, one network per period, each started from the end state \((\theta_{\rm PINN},\dot\theta_{\rm PINN})\) of the previous one, a procedure called time marching. The error is measured against the RK4 reference with step \(10^{-3}\).

def pend_pinn(t_a, t_b, y0, n_col, seed=0):
    # PINN theta_PINN(t) for theta'' + omega_p^2 sin(theta) = 0 on [t_a, t_b] with theta(t_a), theta'(t_a) = y0
    net_p, d1_p, d2_p = make_net(t_a, t_b)
    t_col = jnp.linspace(t_a, t_b, n_col)
    def loss(p):
        r = jax.vmap(lambda t: d2_p(p, t) + OMEGA_P2 * jnp.sin(net_p(p, t)))(t_col)
        return jnp.mean(r**2) + (net_p(p, t_a) - y0[0])**2 + (d1_p(p, t_a) - y0[1])**2
    p, h = train(loss, init_mlp(jax.random.key(seed), SIZES), N_ADAM, N_LBFGS)
    th = lambda t: np.asarray(jax.vmap(lambda s: net_p(p, s))(jnp.asarray(t)))
    om = lambda t: np.asarray(jax.vmap(lambda s: d1_p(p, s))(jnp.asarray(t)))
    return th, om, h

On one period all three seeds reach maximal errors between \(3.7\times10^{-5}\) and \(6.9\times10^{-5}\). On two periods all three seeds converge to the same function with the loss \(3.1\times10^{-3}\), which leaves the solution at \(t=1.95\) and settles at the upright position \(\theta=-\pi\). The equilibrium portion has a small residual, but the preceding transient need not obey the equation accurately. The loss averages the squared residuals, so the number of collocation points does not itself increase their weight relative to the initial conditions. Since the initial-position penalty is nonnegative and part of the total loss, its error is at most \(\sqrt{0.0031}\simeq0.056\). The failed fit therefore cannot be the constant curve \(-\pi\) that ignores its initial position. A small average loss can coexist with a large trajectory error. On four periods seeds 0 and 1 reach errors of \(6.5\times10^{-2}\) and \(5.2\times10^{-2}\), and seed 2 fails in the same way with an error of 5.1. Failures of this kind are documented for PINNs and attributed to the optimisation of the residual loss [Krishnapriyan et al., NeurIPS 2021, arXiv:2109.01050]. Training one network per period avoids the large failure in the tested run, and the error grows from \(6.9\times10^{-5}\) in the first window to \(1.4\times10^{-3}\) in the fourth, since every window starts from the slightly wrong end state of the previous one. In this recorded run the four trainings take 68 s, and RK4 with \(h=0.01\) integrates the same four periods in \(3.0\times10^{-4}\) s. Wang, Sankaran and Perdikaris reach a similar ordering in time within a single network by weighting the residual at later times by the size of the residual at earlier times [arXiv:2203.07404]. Accuracy, inverse problems and optimisation limits

A physics-informed neural network represents the solution of a differential equation by a multilayer perceptron and trains it on the mean squared residual of the equation at collocation points, with the derivatives from automatic differentiation, plus penalty terms for the initial and boundary conditions and for data. On the damped oscillator the network with 2209 parameters reaches a maximal error of \(1.2\times10^{-3}\) after 10000 Adam and 3000 L-BFGS iterations. A fourth-order Runge–Kutta integration with \(h=0.2\) is more accurate and more than a million times faster, and the adaptive Tsit5 solver of diffrax reaches \(8\times10^{-12}\) with a few thousand evaluations, so for a forward initial-value problem of a low-dimensional ODE the PINN does not compete with a classical solver.

The residual becomes useful in combination with data. Twelve noise-free samples of the first 1.3 periods suffice to reconstruct the oscillation over the whole window with an error of \(3.0\times10^{-4}\), where a fit of the same network to the data alone drifts away by 1.2 outside the data window. With noisy observations and an unknown damping the same loss estimates \(c\) together with the trajectory, \(0.4009\pm0.0056\) over five noise realisations for the true value 0.4, against \(0.3998\pm0.0040\) for a least-squares fit of the analytical solution.

The heat equation \(\partial_tu=D\partial_x^2u\) in a rod repeats both findings for a partial differential equation. With known \(D\) the PINN reaches \(1.2\times10^{-3}\), while a Crank–Nicolson solver on a grid of \(20\times20\) points is more accurate in a time about a million times shorter. With \(D\) unknown and four thermometers read at 20 times with noise \(\sigma=0.01\), the PINN estimates \(D=0.09984\pm0.00058\) for the true value 0.1, the same within the noise as a least-squares fit through the Crank–Nicolson solver, \(0.09978\pm0.00052\), and returns the whole temperature field with an error of a few times \(10^{-3}\). The classical fit is again much faster.

The pendulum shows the main weakness of the method. On one period the PINN agrees with RK4 to below \(10^{-4}\), while on longer windows the fitted curve can depart from the reference and approach the upright equilibrium despite a small average loss. Training one network per period avoids this failure in the tested run at a cost many orders of magnitude above that of RK4. These conclusions refer to the networks, budgets and problems studied here.
